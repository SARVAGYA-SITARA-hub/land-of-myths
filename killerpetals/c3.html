A Fish Too Big?

1,789 words
Reading on

I was in the sea, and every breath I did not take was a decision I was taking, and the surface was a bright thing very far above me that I appeared to be arguing with instead of swimming toward.

The sea knows I lied to it.

That was the thought, when thought came. Not the rope. Not the fishermen. Just: there was a night on this water when I told it a prince had drowned, and the water has a long memory and no sense of humour, and it had been waiting for me to fall back into it.

My chest gave out first. Then my mouth did on its own, and the sea came in, and the world went quiet and specific, and I was done.

*

"Are them fishes to your likin'?"

A deep, pleased voice, from somewhere behind the man who was looking at me.

The man looking at me did not answer straight away. He had a cloth wound round his head against the sun, and the sort of face that had been in this business a long time and had stopped finding anything about it odd.

"Uh — yea. But one of 'em is too big."

"Ooh. How big are we talkin'?"

"Very big."

"Is it this big?"

"No. Even bigger."

"Bigger."

Something put its weight on the deck planking. Planking creaked in a way I could feel through my cheek. I could not lift my head far enough to see who was coming — only boots, and the shadow of a very great deal of man.

The boots stopped at my face.

"T-THIS!"

He was enormous. His shirt was losing a war it had been losing for years. He looked down at me and his eyes went round with something that was not yet a thought.

"WE ARE GONNA FEAST TODAY, BOYS!"

The deck answered with a noise that would have moved me to terror if I had had the energy to be terrified. I made an attempt at a voice and it came out as something the sea had left behind.

"Could someone get me out of this."

Silence. Then, carefully:

"...Oh? The fish is talkin'?"

"I am not a fish."

"He's a fish," said the enormous man, delightedly, to nobody in particular, "he's a talking fish."

*

"So you fell in the sea while huntin', you say."

The enormous man — Capitan Ostrand — had put me in a heel of bread in the shade of the main hatch and sat himself down opposite like a man settling in for the whole afternoon.

"Yes," I said, with the best of my memory and none of my truth.

"Huntin' what?"

"...Birds."

"At sea."

"They were flying low."

He turned a piece of bread over in his hands. He had the patience of a man who has heard every lie and has come to enjoy them as a kind of performance.

"Hands," he said, around the bread.

Somebody behind me took my wrists. I had held them so that the rope marks were underneath, and I had been careful not to put them where they would catch the sun, and none of it made any difference, because the man behind me had been a rope-maker's apprentice before he was anything else and could read a wrist like a page.

"Rope," said the man behind me.

"How long, do you reckon?"

"Two days. Maybe three. They used the good stuff, too — see how it bites."

Ostrand finished his bread and looked at me with an entirely new interest. The interest of a man who has found something in his catch that is worth more than fish.

"What's your name, boy?"

I did not answer at once, and it was not caution. I had been dead since morning under the name I was born with, and it had not been worth much to anyone living. The moment I opened my mouth with it I would be a prince again — hunted again, and there is no country on the eastern continent where a hunted prince keeps his own name for long.

A fisherman two boats over was cursing somebody called Tanno about a net.

"Tanno," I said.

"Tanno who?"

"Just Tanno."

Ostrand held my eyes for a while, and something went across his face and was put away again. Then he got up, brushed the crumbs off himself, and said to the fishermen in a perfectly level voice, in a perfectly ordinary tone:

"Keep the fishes. Toss the brat back."

"Yes, Capitan."

"Wait." My voice came out flat because there was nothing left in me to put behind it. "I can't swim."

The deck went quiet. Not nervous quiet — interested quiet. The man behind me shifted. Somewhere a gull had opinions about all of this.

Ostrand turned around.

He looked at me for a length of time I could not measure, and whatever had gone across his face before came back and stayed a while, and I became aware of the fact that I was thirteen years old and covered in salt and half somebody's lunch, and that I was, despite all of it, being looked at the way a man looks at a thing he is considering taking with him.

"Well," he said at last. "We'd best find a port, then."

*

"Are you serious? I beg you to reconsider."

"HARD TO PORT, NETTLE — you worthless sack of root vegetables. HARD TO PORT."

"I am at port—"

"ARE YOU ARGUING WITH ME IN FRONT OF THE BRAT—"

"Capitan, the sheets—"

"LET THE CURTAINS GO WILD."

The sails broke open all at once and the whole ship leaned and the crew howled and went up the ropes and the deck came alive with men who were suddenly all in the way of each other and none of them in the way of the ship, which is the only skill that matters at sea. The hull moved under me and my stomach moved somewhere with it.

I had been put in the small boat — Nettle's idea, and a good one, and I would have said so to his face if I had had any air — with two oars, no sail, and a length of rope coiled at my feet that nobody had bothered to take away.

I rowed.

I did not decide to. The ship pulled away and the gap became a thing I could measure and the measuring was all I had, so I rowed. My palms went from burning to tearing to a kind of numb wet wrongness I made myself not look at.

The ship did not stop. Of course it did not stop. I watched the dark shape of it get smaller until it was a thumbprint, and then until it was a smudge, and then until it was the thing I had been looking at for some minutes without it being anything at all.

My hands came off the oars before I told them to.

"Should I just go back?"

I looked behind me. The sun had gone. The sea was the colour of the inside of a mouth.

I opened my palms, and looked, properly this time, and understood that I was not going to be doing any more of that.

I closed my eyes.

"There is no use running, Your Highness."

"Your Highness, my apologies — I have to be somewhere before noon."

Her voice. Silver in the corridor. Going away from me faster than I could walk, because she had somewhere to be and I had been the last thing on the way.

"All the countries of the eastern continent are now in our grasp. Who could even imagine — their country's ruler swapped with our people."

My father, laughing, alone in a room, at a mirror. Laughing too much. Then stopping. Then turning his head.

Then something warm came down on the backs of my hands, and I opened my eyes, and there were two small dark eyes just above the gunwale, looking at me the way the world looks at you when you have stopped being interesting to it.

A dolphin.

She held my gaze for a while, which no dolphin has ever done to me since. Then she turned her snout — deliberately, and with what I would swear was a sense of occasion — toward the bottom of the boat.

There was a fish there. A small one, out of the sea, shaking itself to pieces against the boards.

"Do you want it?"

I picked it up. It was cold and frantic and it fit in my hand. I held it out over the rail.

She took it the way you take something from a friend. Then she went around and around the boat in a tight circle, delighted, like a dog that has been given a stick and does not know what to do with the joy of it.

I laughed. It came out of me entirely without permission, and it was, I want to be clear, the first true thing I had done since I woke up in a cave.

And then, under the laugh, the other thing.

It came the way a smell comes — the rope round my wrists, the drums, the old man's hand raised — and gone again before I could take hold of it, and I was left with the taste of iron in my mouth and no reason for it on this boat. I had asked for power. I remembered asking. I had asked with my head coming down on the block and had got — this. A boat. A ruined pair of hands. A dolphin.

Where was the power, then. What had it cost, and who had paid it, and why could I not remember the price being agreed.

And then I saw the rope.

It was still there, coiled at my feet, the way it had been the whole time, and I had been looking at it with my hands and not with my head.

She was swimming a furrow in the water beside the boat. She would do it again, because she was a dolphin and because she had decided she liked me, and she would do it until the light went and after, in whatever way a dolphin decides things.

She had no idea. She had absolutely no idea what she was looking at.

I put the rope over the side.

"Heh," I heard myself say. "Heh — heh —"

And it got away from me then, and I laughed until my ribs hurt, bent over the wet boards while the dolphin circled and looked up at me with those two dark eyes, and the last of the sun went, and the sea held absolutely still and let the world know what a liar I was.
