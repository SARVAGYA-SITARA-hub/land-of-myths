Killer Petals · Chapter 1

Altar of Gods

2,385 words
Reading on

"Phew."

My breath came back at me off the rock, loud, and I clamped my mouth shut on it. My right hand was closed around a crack in the cliff face about as wide as a bad promise, and the rest of me was hanging off it.

I looked down. That was a mistake. The sea was doing something patient down there, the way it does when it has all night.

I looked up instead. The sun had already stepped off its stage; the last of it was a stain on the clouds and nothing more. Which left one way.

"Hah. One step at a time."

I found another crack by feel. Then another. The cliff was wet where the tide reached and slick above that, and my fingers had gone from pain to a kind of ringing numbness, which I knew was worse.

If they see me I am dead for sure this time.

Except they wouldn't see me. They thought I was fish food. That was the whole point of the coin, the arm, the water — and it was a very good point, and it would keep being a good point right up until my hands failed and the sea found out I had lied to it.

I moved right, along a ledge no wider than my foot, keeping my voice down, keeping everything down.

"Ahh—"

A lump of stone went out from under my boot and I squeaked like something small being stepped on, and then I was still, cheek against the wet rock, listening to it fall. It fell for a long time. Then it stopped.

One step. Another. The throbbing in my fingers got a rhythm to it, and then a voice, and the voice said things about letting go that I did not agree with.

Then I saw light.

Dim. Orange. Coming out of the rock — a hairline of it, gone when I blinked and back when I didn't. Anyone could have missed it. Anyone not currently bargaining with their own hands for another five minutes of life.

I was so delighted I forgot the fatigue, which is a thing the body lets you do exactly once.

"Just a bit more."

I got my boot onto something. I lowered my weight onto it one inch at a time. I reached, and found an edge, and it was the edge of a mouth in the cliff, and I grabbed it and hauled and the last of me came up over the lip and I flung myself inside and landed flat on hard bedrock like a sack of things nobody wanted.

"Ah — ah — ah."

I lay there. The strength went out of me all at once, taking the pain with it, and the exhaustion came down over me like a blanket somebody had been warming for exactly this.

Above the ledge were the marks of a hand that had done this before me — a wide crack in the rock, worn smooth at the edges, big enough to fold a person into. A fire had been made there once. Recently. The ash was still soft.

Somebody lived up here. Or had.

That should have frightened me. I was too tired to be frightened; I filed it, the way you file a sound in a house you're too exhausted to go and check.

Despite the pain I crawled my way further in, because the sea was still making its patient noise below and I did not trust it.

"Slurp — yuck."

I spat the seawater back out of my mouth and looked around like a man looking for one specific thing and willing to be humiliated for it. Then I saw the earthen pot, sitting at the other edge of the cave, in that patient way pots have.

I was on it before I had decided to move. Lid off. Water. Not seawater — water, clear, with the cold coming off it.

I got both arms around the pot and lifted it and drank like something that had crawled out of the sea, which I was.

It went down like a waterfall into a bottomless pit.

When I finally set it down my hands were shaking so hard the pot rang on the stone. I slumped back against the wall of the cave and looked out at the last grey line of the sea, and the night coming on, and the rain coming with it — soft at first, then not soft, drumming on the rock mouth of the cave like a hundred knuckles.

Somewhere down there, in the dark, a man was telling another man that a prince had drowned. I hoped he was very convincing.

I did not mean to sleep. I remember thinking that I should stay awake and listen for the sound of someone climbing. I remember thinking that the water tasted like iron. And I remember the rain, and the wet, and how warm the cave was against my back.

Then nothing.

*

"HOLA!"

The man in front sang it out and the carrying pole dug into my chest, and I came awake upside down with my wrists tied to a beam and my ankles tied to the same beam and the whole forest going by at a walking pace.

"ULA ULA ULA!" someone called back from behind us.

They were not soldiers. That was the first thing I understood and the least useful. They wore hides over one shoulder and nothing over the other, and their arms were painted from wrist to elbow in a pattern I did not know — lines and dots, like a road map of somewhere I had never been. Bone through the ear. A cord of small teeth around the throat, and I made myself stop counting the teeth.

"RELEASE ME, I SAID, RELEASE—"

I kept it up until my throat hurt. Nobody looked at me. Not once, not even the ones carrying me — the pole was just a thing they were doing with their shoulders. In the end I stopped, because a prince learns early that a performance without an audience is only noise, and because I needed the strength.

We walked a long time. Long enough that I stopped being able to tell the direction by the light. Then the trees opened out and the ground went down and the sound changed — a great hollowness ahead of us, all echoing — and we came out into the dome.

The dome was the roof of a world that had been put underground.

Circular paths spiralled the walls, one above another, stairs running between them, and everywhere tunnel mouths opened off into dark — more passages than I could count, all of them converging on the floor. It was built the way a thing is built when whoever built it expected to be underground for a long time.

The air was warm and wet and smelled of smoke and tallow and something green. Under all of it was a smell I could not place and did not like. My head was ringing from hanging upside down for so long that I could not be sure how long I had been looking before I saw the middle of the room — and once I saw it, I could not look away.

In the middle of the dome stood a stone, grey and ancient, and on the stone sat the rose.

It was a rose the way a funeral is a gathering. Red, but wrong red — not the red of wine or of blood freshly spilled, though I had seen both — a red that seemed to come from inside and go outward, as if the thing were breathing and had decided to let me see it do it.

For a moment there was no forest behind me, no pole under my arms, no rope. Just the rose, and me, and a quiet I could not hear anything through.

The stone it sat on was cracked from top to bottom and green with something that had been growing in the cracks a long time before the tribe or the tunnel or the kingdom above us. Garlands hung down its sides — dried, some of them, and some not — and fruit, and skins, and small bundles of something I chose not to identify.

They had been bringing things here for a long time. The stone had the look of a thing that had received.

"Ola, hula huh u" — the words barked out of the old man in the mask, and the two carrying me came to a halt and dumped me on my knees.

I twisted, got my head up. More of them were arriving from the passages, drums starting from maybe twenty places at once and settling into one rhythm, the way a crowd will find a heartbeat. They untied me and the relief in my arms was so bright it made my eyes water.

Then hands on me, and a bowl at my mouth, and I was drinking something warm and thick before I could decide not to, and my throat closed against it and they kept pouring. Then they were pulling at my clothes, and I fought that — I remember fighting that — and I lost, and they dressed me in cloth that was not cloth exactly, made of small branches bound together with something flexible and alive that was still moving, tied at the shoulder and the hip.

Cold air on parts of me that had never been cold in public. The drums getting faster. My hands tied behind my back now. And the stone, and the rose, coming closer with every step, and my good leg giving out on the third step and the rest of the steps made on the other one.

"Gosh — how many times do I have to tell you to stop sneaking out of the castle—"

My mother's voice, out of nowhere, exactly as Sabine had said it a hundred times — cross, and fond, and about to laugh. I had hated that tone. I would have given the whole kingdom I no longer had to be hearing it right then.

"I heard you skipped the basic swordsmanship class again."

And my father. Looking at me the way he used to look at me — which was not angry, which was the thing I had never been able to explain to anyone, because he never once raised his voice and somehow it was always worse.

"Greetings, Your Highness."

A girl my age, in the corridor outside a hall I could not name, with the sun behind her.

I had stared at her for so long that the corridor had gone quiet around us. I remember that. I remember she had been amused and I had been eleven kinds of ruined and I had said something, and it had been the wrong thing, and she had known it was the wrong thing and been kind about it.

*Lynna.*

That namesake of hers was not here. The corridor was not here. What was here was a stone with a crack in it and a rose on top of it and my knees on a platform of rough stone and a ringing in my ears that had no business being a sound.

My eyes blurred. The tears came and kept coming, and I did not wipe them, because my hands were tied, and because I was busy understanding a thing I had spent a great deal of effort not understanding: that I was not going to see any of them again. Not the corridor. Not the tone. Not the girl with the sun behind her.

I ground my teeth and breathed out through my nose and put my face back together by force of will, and it was a very good face, and it fell apart again anyway.

They pressed me down onto the platform. The old man in the mask came round to my side and began the hymn.

I cannot give you the words. I have tried since, and the best I can say is that they were in an old language, and that the old language had been shaped for something else before it was made to do this. It went up and down in no pattern I could follow. It called, and something answered — not aloud. I felt it answer, in the stone under my knees, the way a staircase feels a foot before the foot arrives.

The rose was listening. That is the plainest way I can put it, and the truest. The rose was listening, and as the hymn went on, the red in it began to move on the inside.

They brought the sword out.

Kord, who was a big man, came out with a blade longer than his arm, and the crowd made the sound a crowd makes when the interesting part is about to start. My teeth went together and rattled. I could not stop them.

And underneath the fear there was something else, and I am not proud of it, and I will not pretend it was not there: I was burning. I hated myself for having run away. Yes. I hated myself for how little of that morning I would ever get to change.

Could I have done something?

What would the palace look like right now — the hall lit, the table set, the whole machine turning without me?

And then the other hands — the ones that have always been in that room. That Sun symbol. I clenched my teeth so hard something at the back of my jaw gave. The corpses. And those fakes standing behind them, calling themselves my parents.

The executioner wiped the blade down with a cloth, unhurried, the way a man wipes a tool he has used before and will use again.

I will kill them.

He took his place at my side. The old man raised his hand to the crowd, and the drums stopped so completely that I heard the river, a long way above us, in the rock.

Every one of them.

The blade came up. The light went along the edge of it and off the ceiling of the dome and out of my sight.

Even if — in the process — I lose myself.

The old man's hand came down.

If only that day I had power.

The blade came down after it.
