Killer Petals · Chapter 2

An Oath of Blood

2,793 words
Reading on

I, or maybe only my head, struck the stone floor and rolled, and came to rest at last against the foot of the old stone.

The last thing I saw was my own body, headless, convulsing — and the fountain of it going up and out and over, endless, and the stone black with it, and the whole dome turning the colour of the inside of a mouth.

Ah.

So that is what kind of red it is.

Then the light went out of my eyes, and the last thing I understood was that I had been wrong about the rose, and that being wrong about the rose was going to matter.

*

Where did it go wrong?

"I apologise, Your Highness, but I have to be somewhere before noon."

She was annoyed. Not the courtly kind, the true kind, and she did not bother to hide it from me, which I had always taken as a compliment and was probably not one.

"Aww. Even if you went in the evening, you'd have plenty of time to get ready."

I had her hand. I had got hold of it somewhere between the corridor and the stair and I was not letting go of it, and she was letting me not let go of it, which was as close to a yes as I had ever got out of her.

She twisted her wrist. Not hard — she never did anything hard, and that was the whole difficulty with her — and my grip came open, and she turned, and went.

Her hair was silver. That is the thing I have kept. Not her face; I have lost the face. The hair going out from her shoulders with the sun on it, and the corridor, and me standing in it with my hand still open, and the distance arriving between us faster than she was walking.

I made a move to follow. She turned her head and looked back down her shoulder at me and did not say anything at all.

Her eyes were the colour of the ice on the palace reservoir in the third month, and they put me exactly where I was standing, and I did not follow her.

If I had told her that day how much I liked her.

Would it have been any different?

*

"How do I look?"

Abel had the expression of somebody who had swallowed something sour and was too well trained to mention it.

"You look absolutely stunning, Your Highness," he said, as a matter of fact.

I smiled at myself in the mirror and turned a shoulder and looked at that, and I was, I want to be fair to the boy I was, worth looking at. "Fuuu. Today I'm going to ask her."

In my head I got down on one knee. In my head I kissed her hand. In my head she laughed the way she laughed when she was not being careful, and said something about how long I had taken about it.

"Your Highness."

"Hmm?"

"This humble servant is known by the name of Abel."

It barely half reached me. I was fixing my collar. I was wondering whether she would be in blue.

I waved him out. He bowed — properly, the full bow, the one the old ones still did — and left, and shut the door quietly behind him, which is a harder thing to do than it looks and which he had been doing for my family for forty-one years.

If I had treated him any better that day. If I had remembered his name.

Would it have been any different?

I do not think so. But I think about it. That is the whole of it — I do not think it would have changed anything, and I think about it.

*

The hall was full and lit and the noise of it came at you in layers — the low one of a hundred conversations, the bright one of glass, and under both of them somebody's musicians being paid not to be interesting.

I stood at the side with a glass I was not drinking and shook it, because it gave my hand something to do.

My fingers were shaking anyway. I had spent an hour arranging for her to be here. Three days arranging for this to be the night. And now the double doors were shut and the room was too warm and the door was taking an unreasonable amount of time about it.

My thumb found the silver coin in my pocket and turned it over, once, twice — the coin Sabine had given me at the first banquet she let me attend, and which she had not said anything about when she gave it, and which I had carried every day since, because it was hers and because I was sentimental and because it was, in the end, the only thing she ever actually handed me.

"Lynna Asherforth and Lord Casimir Vane," the herald said — Perrin, who had announced every name in that hall for nineteen years and had never once enjoyed it.

I knew what that meant. Everyone in that hall knew what that meant. When the herald gives two names together — a man's and a woman's — he is not announcing two guests. He is announcing a couple.

Cut through the door and the dark and the noise of the hall — see it happen and do not be able to stop it — a rose growing where a hand should be —

I blinked.

My heart went down like a stone in a well, and I did not hear it land, and the glass in my hand was very cold and very steady and I was not.

"Ow."

I took my thumb out of my pocket and it was red, and the coin had a crescent of me on its edge that I could see, and I laughed — I actually laughed, standing alone in a hall with a glass of wine and my whole future walking in through the double doors — because the thing I had been made to do was cut myself and not notice.

The doors opened.

Lynna was in blue. She was in blue and she was walking beside a young man who matched her stride without seeming to try for it, and her hand was hooked in the crook of his elbow, and she was smiling at him.

It was just blue, I thought. Everyone said blue. I have spent three days imagining what colour and everyone says blue and I am looking at it and there is nothing to it —

"Lynna," I said.

She was passing me. I had not intended to say it. She did not turn her head. She was smiling at Casimir Vane, and the crowd was closing around them, and I could not for the life of me have told you whether she had not heard me or had decided not to.

Maybe that was why she had to leave at noon.

*

The guests had begun collecting their cloaks. I had spent the last hour of the party deciding to leave and then having another glass, and that was the state of mind I was in when I started across the floor.

I was going to ask her something. I had not settled what. I had the impression that if I did not ask her something, tonight, then the door would close and the thing would be decided and I would be a man who had been in the room when it happened.

"Lynna."

Casimir Vane got to her first.

My feet stopped.

The noise went out of the hall. Not all of it — but the layer that had been around her, the chattering layer, it thinned, and the people near her went still in the way people do when they have understood something before they have been told it.

He went down on one knee.

He lifted her hand, and with his other hand took out a ring.

"Oh, my—"

"How bold."

The whispers spread out from them in rings, the way a struck note spreads.

"Will you accept this ring as the sign of our promise?"

The hall came apart into cheering.

Lynna blushed. Her ears went red — her ears, of all things — and the blush moved down along her jaw while she lifted her head and looked around at the crowd with an expression I could not read.

"Y-you. Here," she said, and glared at him.

Her glare at him was not her glare at me. That is the thing that is still somewhere behind my ribs, even now, even after everything: she can look at him like that and be happy about it, and she never once looked at me like that.

She tilted her head this way and that, trying the crowd on for size, and made a small production of being uncertain, and did not step backwards.

I could have stopped it.

I want to say that clearly, because it took me years to be honest about it: I could have stopped it. A word would have done it. Half a word, in the right voice, into the right ear. I had it — I was a prince, and I was her friend, and I had the right, if anyone did.

My legs did not move. Not out of fear — that came later, that is not what happened in the room. My legs did not move because I was standing in a ballroom watching the girl I loved be happy, and some small unbearable honest part of me had noticed the second word and could not look away from it.

She was happy. She was happy with him. She had never been unhappy in any way I could fix.

When she did not refuse, he smiled at her — he had a good smile, I hate that he had a good smile — and her ears went red again, and he slid the ring onto her finger.

If I had had a bit of courage that day.

Would it have been any different?

*

Where did I go wrong?

What should I have done instead?

*

A pot, an earthen pot. Anew which lay a moment ago, then broken in pieces on ground

Another appeared anew beside it-

And yet when I saw another, then another and so on came in front of me, each differing in their demeanour…….

And yet when I held my gaze, a rose appeared out of nowhere beneath the broken pot, the rose grew and grew in no time taking the pieces of broken pot and covering itself as to recreate what hath been destroyed.

And yet when he made me wonder, went forth its roots devouring the vases which were anew withal he took a piece of each vase adding another to the vase he wore akin to a cloak.

Cared nought the rose for he devoured when it smote the vases different.

Weeping sung whence it went, leaving only annihilation in it’s wake.

*

I opened my eyes.

I did not believe it. That is the part I want on the record, because everyone since has asked me what it felt like and I have never once said it plainly: I did not believe it, and I was relieved, and the relief was the first thing and the disbelief came after, and I have had a long time now to think about what it means that I got them in that order.

My fingers moved. My legs moved, and my chest, and my neck, which I put my hand to, and which was still attached.

Not in the wrong place. Attached. With a pulse going through it, fast and uneven, entirely mine.

I lay still and let it beat and did not open my eyes, and while I was not looking I found the thing I had been avoiding. My fingers went up the side of it, past the jaw, to where the blade had come down.

There was no seam. Not a scar, not a ridge, not the raised line a wound leaves when it heals badly. The skin was whole and unmarked and it was not even the skin of a boy who had been dead an hour — it was the skin of someone who had never in his life been cut at all.

That was worse than the sword. I could argue with a sword.

I sat up.

A dream, I thought, and I believed it, and it was the stupidest and most human thing I have done in my life. The water was bad. That is all. I have slept in a bad place and had a bad dream and now I am awake and it is still night and nothing has happened.

And having decided it, I committed. The way a man commits to a leg he has just been told is broken — all his weight, at once, so that nobody in the room can see him hesitate.

It was water. It was the altar and the drums and the drinking and the fear, and the mind does odd work when it has been frightened past its limit, and I had known men who woke from a bad night convinced of a whole second life. This was that. This was the cave still, in some way I had not untangled yet, and the reef outside, and the morning coming.

I was so pleased with this. I want that on the record too. I sat up in a room that smelled of tallow and old smoke and felt the relief go through me warm as drink, and thought: nobody cut my head off. Nobody at all. Just a bad dream and a sore neck and a great deal of luck.

And I had been dead since the sun was up, and I had not once, in all of that, wondered how I had got off the altar. That is the part I am ashamed of. Not the fear. The fact that relief came first and thinking came after, and thinking, when it finally arrived, arrived wearing the wrong face.

And then I looked at my clothes.

Branches, bound together with thread. Tied at the shoulder and the hip. The rope had been cut off my wrists and the marks of it were still there, going around, going purple.

The dream had dressed me.

I looked around. The dome was there — the circular paths, the stairs, the tunnel mouths. The altar was there. The sword was there, lying on the rough stone platform where someone had put it down and not come back for it.

And nothing else was.

No tribe. No drums. No old man in the mask. No body — no, that is wrong, there was a body, and I looked at it, and I am not going to describe it here, and afterwards I sat down and was for a while very far away.

There was no blood on the stone, either. That was the part my mind would not hold. The stone was dry. The crack in it was dry. Whatever had happened there had been finished for longer than a night.

Somebody had been here and tidied.

I turned around.

The altar. The sword. The empty room.

The Rose was gone.

And I stood there in clothes made of branches, in a room where I had been killed, with my hand on a head that should not have been on my shoulders, and understood — slowly, the way you understand that the noise outside is not weather but the house coming down — that I had been given something back, and that I did not know who had given it to me, or what they had wanted, or what I had agreed to while I was dead.

Something had got me up off that floor. Something had dressed me.

I could feel it still, under everything, the way you can feel a word on the tip of your tongue — the warm, patient wrongness of being owed.

You are welcome, something said. Not in words. Not in any way I could have repeated to a living person. Something said it anyway, and I felt it in the stone through the soles of my feet, and I understood that a debt had been opened in this room that night, and that I was standing in it, and that it was mine.

I put the sword under my arm — because I was not going to be defenceless twice in one week, and because it was there and I was not thinking about its owner — and I walked out of that dome, and it took me two days to find the coast.
