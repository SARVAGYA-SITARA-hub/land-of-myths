Killer Petals · Chapter 4

Stop It, Brat

703 words
Reading on

The sun was high. The weather was lovely.

That part was for me. None of the rest of it was.

Here is what happened, in the order the ship experienced it.

Capitan Ostrand came out on deck with his cup, as he did every morning, and admired the weather, as he did every morning, and drank his tea with a noise like a bucket going down a well. The gulls were up. The water was that flat blue a sailor will tell you he likes and does not like.

Then he saw something.

No. He saw someone. And that someone was—

"WOOOOOOOO."

I was sitting in the small boat alongside, at a cruising speed, steering with one oar and trailing the other like a man who had invented the idea.

Behind me, at the end of four hundred feet of rope, a dolphin was doing this to me on purpose.

"HOW—" Ostrand set his cup down. Then he picked it up again and drank from it and set it down. "How in the great three seas is this possible."

"I told you, Capitan," said Nettle, appearing at his elbow with the tan cloth wound round his head, "when the black cats cut the route you're meant to be taking, you turn back."

"I — " Ostrand looked at the boat, and at me in the boat, and at the dolphin, and at the sky, checking. "I should've listened."

"Well." Nettle considered it. "If we find a port, we can toss the kid there. Put him down on the quay and be off about our business."

"Aye," said Ostrand, and turned to look at me properly.

That was the first I noticed it. He had been talking about me all morning in that voice of his: big, cheerful, loud enough to be heard on the other ship of a convoy — and then it was quiet for too long, and Nettle looked at him, and Nettle's face changed.

Ostrand's eyes had gone somewhere. He was standing on his own deck with his cup in his hand and his ship under his feet and his crew going about their work and there was nobody in him.

"Capitan?" Nettle said.

Nothing.

"Capitan."

Nothing. The cup dipped and tea went over the rim of it onto his hand and he did not seem to notice that it was hot.

"CAPITAN. LOOK AT ME."

Nettle did not shout it like a man shouting at a captain. He shouted it like a man calling somebody back from a long way off. The crew stopped. The whole deck stopped, in the way a deck stops when every man on it has seen this before and knows exactly how long to keep their eyes elsewhere.

Ostrand turned his head.

His eyes were wet.

"It was not your mistake," Nettle said. He had both hands up, not touching him, the way you hold your hands up to a horse. His shoulders were shaking and I could not tell which of them it was coming from. "Nothing ever was. Say it back to me. Say it."

Ostrand looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked at me instead, over Nettle's shoulder — at me, in a rowing boat, tied to a dolphin, wet to the skin and grinning like an idiot — and something in his face did a thing I understood very well and did not like at all.

It looked at me the way a man looks at something that has arrived when he was sure nothing would ever arrive again.

He did not say anything. He walked to the cabin door and opened it and went in and shut it, and the brass of the lock going over was the loudest sound on the water.

Nettle stood there with his hands still half-raised.

The crew found things to do.

Then Nettle lowered his hands, and his eyes came down, and stayed down for a while, and when he lifted them again they landed on me.

And in that moment I stopped being a joke in a small boat and became something else to somebody — though what, exactly, I would not learn for a very long time, and should not have wanted to.
