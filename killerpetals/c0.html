Prologue

681 words
Reading on

Snap — branches, leaves, something behind me shearing loose. I did not look. Looking cost time, and time was the only thing I had left.

"There is no use running, Your Highness."

An arrow went past my right ear close enough that I felt the air of it and nothing else.

I gasped. My heart was a drum in a closed room, and the whole room was my skull.

Sweat had soaked through my collar and down my spine. My legs were going. Not yet — not while he could still see me.

"No." I pushed. If I stopped here I was a body on a forest floor and nothing more.

Running this long starts showing. First the breath goes ragged, then the hearing, then the thoughts. The wind shoved past me, and I went through the brush and the vines like something being chased, because I was.

And then the trees simply stopped.

The sea. A cliff, and past it the whole grey turning of the water, and the sun going down behind it in that fat, unhurried way that says the day is finished and the world has decided about you already.

I turned.

Two of them. Black from throat to boot, breathing hard, and pleased about it.

I put my left hand in my pocket and rubbed the bronze coin with my thumb, and while my face did nothing at all my eyes went over the cliff edge, the vine, the gap, the water. Four things. One of them would work.

"There is no use running, Your Highness," Brant said again, and grinned, because he had said it twice now and the second time is always better for a man like that.

"You say that like I haven't just cost you more than half your squad."

The grin faltered. His brows came down. Good. Men who are ashamed make mistakes faster than men who are confident.

Two facts, then. The good one: I had found a way off this cliff. The bad one: he had found it too, half a heartbeat after me, and he was bigger and he had a sword.

I looked at the vine hanging off the tree to my right a moment too long. Deliberately. Let him look at it with me.

He did. Then he looked back at me, and I watched him understand, and I watched the understanding arrive too late to be useful.

"Stop him!"

I was already running.

The one with the dagger came at me from the side, fast, better than I had counted on, and for a moment we were going to arrive at the cliff at the same time and only one of us had thought about it beforehand.

So I stopped running.

I turned into him. Close — closer than a prince should ever be to a hired blade — and let him see my eyes go wet, and put everything I had ever learned about being looked at into one whispered sentence.

"I'm interested in men."

"Y-you — what?"

He stopped. Not his feet; his whole self, the way a man stops when the world has just said something that does not fit in it. His eyes went wide and then, because the mind will do anything rather than hold two impossible things, his eyes closed.

The coin took him on the cheekbone.

I had the vine in my right hand before he had finished flinching, and my left hooked through his elbow, and then there was no more cliff under either of us and the sea came up at me like a wall with opinions.

Above, the swordsman reached the edge and looked down and heard a snap — the vine, giving — and then a splash, and then bubbles, and then not even bubbles.

Brant stood there a while. He was a thorough man. When there was nothing left to look at he turned around and went to tell Aldric that the last of the royal line of Asherforth had drowned in the Estmark Sea with a bronze coin in his pocket and a stranger's arm around his neck.
