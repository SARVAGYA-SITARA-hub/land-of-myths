Against the Heavens · Chapter 7

A Change of Direction

2,721 words
Reading on

The Shuri family was preparing to move.

Or, more accurately, they were discussing whether they should move.

Their belongings were few enough that everything they owned could be packed into several large trunks, a collection of cloth bundles, and two wooden boxes that had seen better days.

It was not the first time they had done this.

Zhen Shuri sat near the doorway, watching his father inspect a worn travel bag.

His mother was sorting through their clothes, separating what they needed from what could be sold or left behind. His younger sibling was busy trying to fit a collection of small toys into a box that was clearly too small.

A familiar scene.

One that the original Zhen had experienced many times.

His family had never been particularly settled. They travelled between towns and settlements, taking whatever work was available. Sometimes his father found employment repairing household devices. Sometimes his mother helped with cleaning, cooking or other domestic work. There were periods when they stayed in one place for several months, and others when they moved again after only a few weeks.

It was an exhausting way to live.

But it kept food on the table.

Zhen looked at the half-packed bags.

Then he spoke.

"Father, I think we should move to the central kingdom."

His father stopped inspecting the bag.

His mother looked up.

Even his younger sibling paused.

For a moment, nobody said anything.

Then his father asked, "The central kingdom?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Zhen had expected that question.

He had been thinking about it for the past two days, trying to find a reason that would not sound suspicious.

Unfortunately, the truth was not something he could simply explain.

Because this place is going to become a disaster zone. Because I remember something terrible happening here. Because one month from now, someone will contract a parasite created by Heaven itself, and that infection will become the beginning of the end.

He maintained a neutral expression.

"I heard there are more workshops and industrial facilities in the central kingdom. The demand for magical engineering work should be higher there."

His father looked at him.

"You heard this from whom?"

"People at the estate."

"Which people?"

Zhen paused.

"...People who talk."

His father stared.

Zhen stared back.

His mother quietly returned to folding clothes.

"That is a remarkably detailed source," his father said.

"I try to be thorough."

"You also tried to repair a regulator with a cracked crystal last week."

"And it worked for nearly four seconds."

"Three."

"Four, if we count the moment before it exploded."

His father closed his eyes.

Zhen decided not to continue defending that particular achievement.

After a moment, his father placed the bag on the floor.

"Listen. The central kingdom is not cheap. There are more opportunities, certainly, but there are also more people competing for them. We would have to find somewhere to stay, and I would need to secure work before we spend what little money we have."

"I know."

"Do you?"

His father did not sound angry.

That made the question more difficult to answer.

Zhen glanced at the wooden boxes.

He knew how much effort his father put into keeping the family afloat. He remembered the careful calculations, the repairs done late at night, and the occasions when his parents quietly went without something so that the children could have it instead.

He did not want to make their lives harder.

But staying here was not an option he could accept.

"I can help," he said.

His father raised an eyebrow.

"I can repair household devices. I can also work in a workshop, even if they only need someone to sort components or assist the technicians. I don't have to attend the academy immediately."

His father remained silent.

Zhen continued.

"And the central kingdom has more opportunities to study engineering. I could learn from people who actually know what they're doing."

"Unlike you?"

"Precisely. I am an excellent example of what happens when someone studies without proper supervision."

His mother gave a small laugh.

His father did not.

But the expression on his face softened slightly.

"Zhen, I know you want to study. I know you want something better than this."

He looked down at the bag.

"But wanting something and being able to afford it are different matters."

"I understand."

"Then don't make promises you cannot keep."

Zhen nodded.

He had no intention of promising that everything would work out.

He could not guarantee a job. He could not guarantee admission to the Central Academy. And he certainly could not guarantee that his memories of the future were accurate enough to save anyone.

But he could try.

"I am not asking you to spend all our savings," he said. "Just give me a chance to find work there. If I cannot find anything, we can reconsider."

His father looked towards his mother.

She had stopped folding clothes.

"What do you think?" he asked.

She considered it for a moment.

"We have moved before. And if there are more opportunities there, perhaps we should try."

His father sighed.

"You two seem to have discussed this already."

"We have," Zhen admitted.

"When?"

"Yesterday."

His father looked at him.

Zhen smiled faintly.

"With myself."

His father rubbed his forehead.

For several seconds, the room remained quiet.

Then he picked up the travel bag again.

"Very well. We will look for work in the central kingdom."

Zhen blinked.

"Really?"

"Do not make me regret it."

"I'll do my best."

"Your best is not always reassuring."

"That's fair."

His father shook his head, but there was no real irritation in his expression.

"Start packing. We will leave once I have arranged the remaining work here."

Zhen nodded.

"Thank you, Father."

His father waved a hand dismissively.

"Save your gratitude. Find us somewhere to stay."

"Yes, sir."

Zhen turned towards the boxes.

For the first time in several days, he felt that something had moved in the right direction.

It was a small step.

But he would take it.

*

The following morning, Zhen woke earlier than usual.

The house was still quiet.

His father had gone out to speak with a local merchant, while his mother was preparing breakfast. His younger sibling was still asleep.

Zhen stepped outside.

The air was cool, and the morning light had only begun to spread across the neighbouring rooftops.

He sat on a low wooden bench.

Then he remembered the tome.

The First Lesson — Perception.

He had read it several times now.

The instructions were simple.

Observe without immediately naming.

Look without assuming that seeing something meant understanding it.

Listen without deciding beforehand which sounds mattered.

At first, he had thought the exercises were almost absurdly basic.

He had spent years studying engineering. He knew how to observe a mechanism, identify its components and understand the purpose of its construction.

Or so he had believed.

The tome seemed to suggest that he had been doing something else entirely.

He looked towards a tree growing beside the house.

Its branches extended unevenly, with several leaves catching the morning sunlight.

Tree.

The word appeared immediately in his mind.

He stopped.

That was exactly what he was supposed to avoid.

He tried again.

The trunk was not perfectly straight. Its surface had several shallow grooves. One branch extended farther than the others, and a smaller one grew beneath it at an awkward angle.

A few leaves had curled at their edges.

One was partially covered by another.

The sunlight reached the upper branches first, leaving the lower portion in shadow.

He continued observing.

The leaves moved.

Not all at once.

Some shifted with the wind, while others barely moved. A thin branch swayed, returned to its original position, then swayed again.

Zhen watched the movement for several minutes.

Then he heard something.

A cart passing along the road.

He turned his attention towards the sound.

The wheels produced a low, uneven rumble. One side seemed louder than the other. The sound grew gradually, reached its peak, and then faded into the distance.

He could distinguish the cart's movement without seeing it.

Interesting.

He closed his eyes.

The sounds around him became easier to separate.

A bird.

A distant conversation.

Someone sweeping the road.

The faint creaking of the wooden gate behind him.

He had heard these things countless times.

He had simply never paid attention to them in this way.

Zhen opened his eyes.

"That old man might actually know what he's doing."

He looked at the tree again.

Then at the rooftops.

Then at the sky.

The world had not changed.

But the way he was examining it had.

He wondered how much of his previous knowledge had been built upon assumptions. How many times had he looked at a magical device and immediately decided what was wrong with it because he recognised a familiar arrangement?

Perhaps he had been overlooking things.

A change in the surrounding temperature.

The direction of the energy flow.

A slight difference in the material.

A component that behaved differently from what its appearance suggested.

He had often approached problems by identifying them first and examining them afterward.

The tome seemed to recommend the opposite.

Observe.

Compare.

Question.

Only then should he attempt to understand.

Zhen leaned back.

"All right, Merlin. Point taken."

He returned inside.

His unfinished regulator design was still on the table.

He picked it up.

For a while, he simply looked at it.

The arrangement of channels.

The damaged conductor.

The position of the crystal.

The small gaps between the components.

He had seen the design dozens of times.

Yet now he found himself examining details he had previously ignored.

He noticed that one of the channels was slightly narrower than the others. He had assumed the difference was insignificant, but the calculations suggested that it might affect the distribution of energy.

He marked the area.

Then he examined the conductor.

Its surface had several tiny irregularities.

He had previously classified it as damaged and unsuitable.

But perhaps the damage was not uniform.

Perhaps only one section needed replacement.

He picked up his pencil.

A new diagram began to take shape.

He did not know whether the revised arrangement would work.

But he was beginning to understand something.

The purpose of perception was not simply to see more.

It was to make fewer assumptions about what he saw.

Three days passed.

The Shuri family made their arrangements, settled their remaining obligations and prepared to leave.

Zhen spent much of his time helping his father, looking for information about the central kingdom and occasionally working on his regulator.

He also continued practising the exercises from Merlin's tome.

Sometimes he observed the movement of clouds.

Sometimes he listened to the sounds of the market.

On one occasion, he spent nearly twenty minutes watching water drip from a damaged roof.

He discovered that there were surprisingly many ways to become bored.

But he also discovered that the more he observed, the more details he noticed.

The water did not fall at perfectly regular intervals.

The drops gathered at the edge of the roof before releasing. Their size varied. Some struck the ground directly, while others landed on the leaves of a nearby plant.

It was ordinary.

Almost painfully ordinary.

Yet he found himself wondering about the forces involved, the shape of the roof, the surface tension of the water and the way the drops changed direction.

He did not need to find a grand meaning in everything.

Sometimes, observing something was enough.

And slowly, the habit began to influence his engineering work.

He started examining discarded components instead of immediately throwing them aside. He compared their behaviour, noted their differences and recorded what he could learn from them.

His progress was not dramatic.

But it was real.

Then, on the evening before their departure, he remembered something.

*

Zhen sat beside the window.

The family had finished packing. Most of their belongings were ready to be loaded onto the transport cart in the morning.

His father was speaking with a neighbour outside.

His mother was preparing the last meal they would have in the house.

Everything was ordinary.

And that was precisely what made the memory so unpleasant.

He remembered the story.

Not all of it.

Never all of it.

The original plot had been enormous, filled with characters, political conflicts, ancient histories and events that he could barely recall in the correct order.

But certain things had remained in his memory.

The disasters.

There were six of them.

Civilisation-level catastrophes, each one different from the last. They were not merely wars or conflicts between powerful individuals. They were events that threatened the existence of entire societies.

The first was biological.

A parasite.

Zhen frowned.

He remembered its appearance only vaguely. More importantly, he remembered what it could do.

It was not an ordinary organism.

It possessed sentience.

It could connect infected organisms through a hive mind, and its capacity to evolve was terrifying. It adapted, changed and developed at a rate that made ordinary biological progression seem almost meaningless.

And it was not a natural occurrence.

It had been created by Heaven itself.

Zhen leaned against the window frame.

"Of course it was."

He remembered the beginning.

The first calamity would strike after the story's opening events, when the Wilhelmshire family was still at the centre of the narrative.

One month.

Approximately one month after the beginning of the original sequence.

His expression grew serious.

He tried to recall the exact circumstances.

Where had it appeared first?

How had it spread?

Was there an initial outbreak, or had it existed unnoticed before anyone recognised the danger?

He could not remember.

The details were frustratingly incomplete.

But one thing stood out more clearly than the rest.

The first person to contract it.

Ajax Wilhelmshire.

Zhen went still.

For several seconds, he simply stared through the window.

The boy with the composed expression.

The young master who had tricked him into polishing a painted spot on a vase.

The governor's disabled son who had seemed far too entertained by the whole incident.

Ajax.

He remembered that name.

In the original story, Ajax was the first victim of the parasite.

And he did not survive the way the original narrative had initially led readers to believe.

Zhen pressed his fingers against his forehead.

"Wait a minute."

The timeline.

The Wilhelmshire estate.

The first infection.

He had been so occupied with his own problems that he had almost forgotten how close the first calamity was.

If his memory was correct, the parasite would emerge in approximately one month.

And Ajax would be the first to contract it.

Zhen looked towards the packed belongings.

Moving to the central kingdom suddenly seemed like a much smaller achievement.

He had managed to persuade his family to relocate.

He had found a way to continue his engineering studies.

He had begun understanding the first lesson of Merlin's tome.

But none of that answered the most important question.

Could he actually prevent what was coming?

He did not know how the parasite infected its first host.

He did not know whether it could be detected before symptoms appeared.

He did not even know whether the original story's events would unfold exactly as he remembered them.

His memories were not a reliable record.

They were fragments.

And fragments could be dangerous.

Zhen looked down at his hands.

He had come to this world with knowledge of its story.

But knowledge was not the same as understanding.

Merlin's first lesson had made that painfully clear.

Perhaps the same principle applied here.

He needed to observe the world as it was, not simply force it to match the story he remembered.

Still...

There was one person he could not afford to ignore.

Ajax Wilhelmshire.

Zhen let out a slow breath.

"I hope I'm wrong."

Outside, his father called for him.

"Zhen! Come help with the last box!"

He looked towards the doorway.

"Coming!"

He stood, but before leaving, he glanced once more at the darkening sky.

The first calamity was approaching.

And somewhere, in a distant estate, a young man was probably spending his evening reading an old diary beneath the light of a rune-powered lamp.

Completely unaware of what awaited him.

The next morning, the Shuri family left for the central kingdom.
