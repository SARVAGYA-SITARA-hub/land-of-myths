Against the Heavens · Chapter 5

The Shuri Household

1,525 words
Reading on

Zhen Shuri was looking at a piece of paper.

It had been the same piece of paper for nearly an hour.

On it were several diagrams, a few calculations, and a rough illustration of what appeared to be a mechanical device.

He had drawn it three times.

The first version had been too complicated.

The second had been too expensive.

The third was... perhaps possible.

He leaned back and examined his work.

"Not bad."

He turned the paper around.

"Actually, no. Terrible."

The device was supposed to be a compact magical regulator. It would take an unstable energy supply and distribute it through several smaller channels, reducing the fluctuations that damaged ordinary artifacts.

At least, that was the idea.

The design itself was not particularly revolutionary. The principles already existed in several forms of magical engineering.

What made his design interesting was its size.

Most affordable regulators were inefficient and bulky. Better ones required carefully manufactured components, rare materials, or a degree of craftsmanship beyond what a commoner could easily afford.

His proposed design attempted to solve the problem through a different arrangement of the internal channels.

It was clever.

At least, he thought so.

Unfortunately, being clever did not make the components appear on his table.

Zhen looked at the small collection of materials beside him.

A few metal plates.

Several damaged conductors.

A cracked energy crystal.

And a regulator component that had probably been discarded because it no longer worked.

He sighed.

"Resources. My greatest enemy."

A knock came from the door.

"Zhen?"

His father's voice.

"Come in."

The door opened.

His father entered, wearing his usual work clothes. He looked tired.

"What are you doing?"

"Preparing something for the academy."

His father glanced at the papers.

"Again?"

Zhen nodded.

His father pulled out the chair opposite him.

"Listen, son. I know you want to attend the Central Academy."

Zhen waited.

"But it is expensive. Even if you get in through merit, there will be travel expenses, equipment costs, and the cost of living there."

"I know."

"You cannot expect us to provide everything."

"I understand."

His father looked at the diagrams.

"Is this what you've been working on?"

"Yes."

"Will it help you get admitted?"

"It might."

His father sighed.

"Zhen, I don't want you wasting your time chasing something that may never happen."

The words were not cruel.

That almost made them worse.

His father was not trying to discourage him out of spite. He was simply looking at the matter from the perspective of a man who had spent his life counting every coin.

The academy was far away.

Its students came from wealthy families, powerful institutions, and countries with resources his family could barely imagine.

Even if Zhen managed to secure admission, how would he afford to stay there?

His father stood.

"Think carefully. You have work at the estate. Keep your position. If you continue studying engineering, you can perhaps find a decent workshop willing to take you as an apprentice."

Zhen lowered his eyes.

"All right."

His father nodded and left.

The door closed.

Zhen looked at the papers again.

For a while, he did not move.

Then he picked up the diagram and folded it.

He knew his father wasn't wrong.

That was the annoying part.

He had memories of the original Zhen Shuri. He knew how much his family struggled, how carefully they managed their money, and how little they could spare.

He also knew what awaited him at the Central Academy.

The main cast.

The events that would shape the future.

And the disasters that would eventually turn this world into something barely recognisable.

He rubbed his forehead.

"Of all the worlds..."

He stopped himself.

Complaining would not solve anything.

He needed a way in.

And he needed resources.

Zhen looked at the broken components on his table.

Perhaps he could approach a local workshop. Perhaps he could offer to repair something in exchange for materials.

Or perhaps he could sell the design.

He frowned.

No. Selling the design without being able to demonstrate it would be difficult.

He needed a working prototype.

He leaned back and stared at the ceiling.

"One month."

He had one month.

It was beginning to sound like a very unpleasant amount of time.

Eventually, exhaustion won.

Zhen pushed the papers aside, lay down, and closed his eyes.

*

The small house appeared once more.

Zhen stood in the middle of the room.

He immediately looked towards the table.

The enormous tome was there.

"Well, look who's back."

He approached it.

The book was exactly as he remembered, its dark cover and reinforced spine unchanged.

He opened it.

The first pages were familiar. The introductory passages, the observations, the diagrams.

He flipped through several sections.

Then he noticed something.

A new passage had appeared.

It was not a continuation of the final entries bearing Merlin's name. Instead, it was a section placed much earlier in the tome, between two groups of notes.

At the top was a simple heading.

The First Lesson — Perception.

Zhen leaned closer.

Before one attempts to understand the world, one must first learn to perceive it.

The eye receives light, but seeing is not the same as understanding.

The ear receives sound, but hearing is not the same as listening.

The mind receives impressions, but impressions are not knowledge.

Zhen turned the page.

There were several exercises.

The first concerned vision.

Observe a single object.

Do not immediately name it. Do not recall what you know about it.

Observe its shape, its edges, the light falling upon it, and the shadows created by its surroundings.

Look long enough to notice what you would normally overlook.

Zhen stared at the illustration accompanying the exercise.

It depicted an ordinary tree.

But the notes surrounding it were extensive.

The writer had recorded the changing position of the branches, the direction of the leaves, the way sunlight passed through gaps in the canopy, and the subtle variations in colour.

The second exercise concerned sound.

Listen to the world without choosing what deserves your attention.

Separate the sounds that overlap. Notice the distance between them. Observe how they change when the source moves.

The third concerned the mind.

The mind is not merely an instrument for remembering.

It is also an instrument for arranging what is perceived.

Observe. Compare. Question. Do not accept the first explanation simply because it is convenient.

Zhen turned another page.

There were more notes.

Some described the movement of water.

Others concerned the behaviour of flames, the formation of clouds, the movement of air, and the patterns of energy in natural environments.

The instructions were not about memorising facts.

They were about learning to notice.

Zhen read a passage twice.

Magic is not made by imagination alone.

Imagination without understanding is a picture without a world.

First perceive. Then imagine. Then believe. And finally, understand.

He stopped.

That was interesting.

Very interesting.

The book did not tell him how to cast a spell.

It did not give him a list of techniques or a collection of ready-made magical formulae.

It was teaching him how to observe the world.

Zhen looked around the small house.

The light entering through the window.

The dust floating in the air.

The faint creaking of the wooden floor beneath his feet.

He had never paid much attention to any of it.

Now, he found himself doing exactly that.

A final note appeared at the bottom of the page.

Do not mistake perception for mastery.

You have only begun to see.

Zhen closed the tome.

He glanced towards the window.

The stars outside seemed unchanged.

Yet he could not shake the feeling that he had been looking at them incorrectly.

Then the room began to fade.

The walls dissolved.

The table disappeared.

The book slipped from his hands.

And the dream ended.

*

Zhen woke up.

He stared at the ceiling.

For a moment, he remained quiet.

Then he sat up and looked at his hands.

"Again."

He glanced towards the table.

His diagrams were still there.

The broken components were still there.

And his problems had not magically disappeared.

He sighed.

"Well, at least the old man is consistent."

He rubbed his eyes.

The two dreams had given him something different.

Ajax had received a path that began with the body and the perception of Qi.

Zhen had received a legacy that began with the senses and the understanding of the world.

Neither had been handed an answer.

Only a first step.

Zhen looked at his folded design.

Then he looked towards the window.

Perhaps there was something worth trying.

He stood up, opened the window, and watched the morning light fall over the neighbouring rooftops.

For the first time in a long while, he did not immediately think about what he could build.

He simply observed.

The movement of the leaves.

The direction of the wind.

The distant sounds of the waking city.

And somewhere beneath all of it, the world continued to move.

Zhen watched for another minute.

Then he remembered that he still had to go to work.

"...Right. Back to being poor."

He hurried to get dressed.
