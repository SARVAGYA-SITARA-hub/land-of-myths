Against the Heavens · Chapter 14

The Quiet Sickness

1,746 words
Reading on

Ajax did not tell the physician about the second sensation.

He had come close. The man had asked whether anything unusual had happened, and Ajax had opened his mouth to describe a pressure that did not belong to him, a presence that had answered once in the dark and then gone quiet.

Then he had looked at the physician's face — tired, harried, already carrying forty other patients — and said instead that he was tired.

It was not entirely a lie.

---

The household settled into a rhythm of waiting.

His mother took up residence in a chair by the window and refused to be moved from it. His father divided his hours between Ajax's room and the communication chamber, where reports arrived at intervals that grew steadily shorter. Elena had been sent to her rooms twice and had returned three times.

By the second morning, the estate had acquired the particular silence of a house with a sick person in it. Servants spoke in lowered voices. The kitchen sent up trays that came back almost untouched. Someone had hung a cloth over the mirror in the corridor, and nobody admitted to doing it.

Ajax found the cloth more disturbing than the illness.

---

The Royal Medical Service sent a second physician on the third day — younger than the first, with the brisk manner of someone who had been told to be efficient and had taken it as a personal instruction.

He asked the same questions in a different order. Then he asked one that was new.

"Have you noticed anything moving?"

Ajax looked at him.

"Moving."

"Under the skin. Beneath the ribs, sometimes the arms. Several patients have mentioned it." The physician's pen hovered. "The senior physicians believe it is a fever artefact. I am asking anyway."

"Why?"

"Because forty people have reported the same artefact," the physician said, "and I do not like coincidences with that many witnesses."

Ajax was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "I have noticed something."

The pen moved.

"Where?"

"Behind the ribs. It does not stay in one place." Ajax considered how much to say, and decided on all of it. "It moves against my breathing. When I breathe in, it goes the other way."

The physician stopped writing.

"Say that again."

"When I breathe in, it goes the other way."

The physician sat down slowly, which Ajax thought was a strange thing to do in the middle of an examination.

"Four days," the physician said. "Four days and nobody has said that. They say it moves. They do not say how."

"Perhaps they have not been paying attention."

"Perhaps." The physician looked at him with an expression Ajax could not read. "Or perhaps you are the first one who knows how to pay attention."

---

That evening the thing inside him moved again.

Ajax had been waiting for it. He had spent the afternoon doing what he had spent a month learning to do — sitting still, letting his attention settle, observing without grasping. His mother thought he was sleeping. He was counting.

The Qi was still there. He could find it now without effort, the way one finds a familiar room in the dark. But it had changed. There were places where the network he had sketched in his diary felt thicker, and places where it felt thin, as though something had been drinking from it.

And beneath it, in the gaps, was the other thing.

It was not Qi. Qi was quiet and everywhere, like the temperature of a room. This was local. It had a here, and the here was behind his ribs, and it moved.

He did not try to touch it. He had learned that much.

He simply watched where it went.

---

It went toward the thin places.

That was the first thing he understood, and it took him most of the night. The creature — he did not yet have a better word, and he resented the word — did not wander. It moved along the network he had drawn in his notebook, and it moved in one direction, and the direction was always toward the places where his Qi was weakest.

It was not attacking him.

It was feeding, and it preferred the parts of him that were already struggling.

Ajax lay in the dark with his eyes open and thought about that for a long time.

---

On the fifth morning, the physician brought news and did not try to soften it.

"The count is above two hundred. There are cases in four districts and two of the outer towns. The Crown has closed the eastern market."

"The food?" Ajax asked.

"The seafood is still the favourite." The physician's mouth twisted. "It is also becoming less convincing. The Exchange has produced documents for every crate, the kitchen records are clean, and three of the affected households never received a shipment at all."

"So it is not the food."

"Something is not the food," the physician agreed. "The senior physicians have not yet decided what."

Ajax looked toward the window. Outside, the orchid trees were shedding petals onto the gravel in a slow, unhurried way that seemed to belong to a different world.

"Physician."

"Mm."

"You said forty people reported movement."

"Forty-one, now."

"How many of them are still alive?"

The physician did not answer immediately.

"Most," he said at last. "But not all of them are still themselves."

---

That was the sentence Ajax carried for the rest of the day.

Not dead. Not recovering. Not still themselves.

He thought about the thing behind his ribs, moving toward the thin places, feeding where he was weakest. He thought about what would be left of a man after enough of that. He thought about how it would feel to be present while it happened — to notice, as he noticed everything, the exact moment his own attention began to belong to something else.

He thought that he would very much prefer not to find out.

---

Elena came to him that evening with a book.

She put it on the bed without ceremony and sat down in the chair their mother had vacated.

"What is it?" Ajax asked.

"Something stupid." She folded her arms. "It's about a knight who gets cursed and has to find a flower. There's a hermit. The hermit is the only good part."

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because you have been lying in this bed for five days being interesting at everyone, and it is exhausting to watch." She looked at the window rather than at him. "And because you cannot read your own books without writing in them, and this one is mine, so I will know if you do."

Ajax turned the book over.

"It has a picture of a flower on the cover."

"It has a picture of the flower."

"It looks like a cabbage."

"It is a very important cabbage," Elena said, and Ajax laughed, and then had to stop because laughing hurt his chest, and Elena's face did something complicated before she rearranged it.

"Elena."

"What."

"Thank you."

She stood up.

"Read the hermit part," she said, and left.

---

He read the hermit part.

The hermit was a minor character who appeared for eleven pages and gave the knight advice that the knight ignored. Ajax read it twice.

The advice was this: you cannot take a thing out of a man that has grown into him. You can only give him something better to hold.

Ajax lay in the dark afterward with the book on his chest and did not sleep for a long time.

He did not know whether the advice was true. He suspected the author had not known either. But it had the shape of something, and he had spent a month learning to notice shapes.

You can only give him something better to hold.

He thought about the thing behind his ribs, feeding on the thin places.

And for the first time since the fever began, he thought: what if I did not try to take it out.

---

The thought frightened him enough that he got up.

He could not walk, so he did not get up in the ordinary sense — he pushed himself upright against the headboard and sat there with his hands in his lap, breathing carefully, while the room settled around him.

Then he closed his eyes and did what he had been doing every night for a month.

He found the Qi.

He found the thing that was not Qi.

And instead of watching it, instead of following it, instead of doing any of the careful, patient, observing things he had taught himself to do —

He thought about his own name.

*Ajax Wilhelmshire.* The second young master. The one who noticed the wiring and the wrong tablecloth and the painted spot on the vase. The one who had asked the stars what crime he had committed, and received no answer, and decided to demand one anyway.

It was not a technique. It was not the scripture, which he had not attempted and did not know how to attempt.

It was simply the most him thing he could think of, held deliberately, the way one holds a lamp up in a dark room.

And for one second — one long, strange, entirely unglamorous second — the thing behind his ribs stopped moving.

---

Ajax opened his eyes.

The room was the same. The cloth was still over the mirror. Somewhere below, a servant was moving a chair.

He was breathing hard, and his chest hurt, and he had no idea what he had just done.

He picked up his notebook and wrote, in handwriting that was worse than usual:

I do not think it is an illness.

He looked at the sentence for a while.

Then he crossed it out — carefully, so it could still be read — and wrote underneath:

I think it is a guest that has not been introduced.

And then, because he was still himself, and because being himself had just done something he did not understand:

I should find out what it wants before it finds out what I want.

---

Outside, the night was very quiet.

In the corridor, the physician on watch had fallen asleep in his chair.

And in a hundred and forty households across the province, people were waking up in the dark and feeling something move beneath their ribs, and reaching for the bell, and finding that their hands did not quite do what they asked.
