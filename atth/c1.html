Against the Heavens · Chapter 1

Ajax Wilhelmshire

1,809 words
Reading on

“Ajax, are you listening?”

Ajax Wilhelmshire raised his eyes.

His mother stood in front of him, one hand resting on the handle of his wheelchair. Beside her stood his father, dressed in a formal tailcoat. His usually immaculate coat had already acquired a few wrinkles, courtesy of the preparations taking place throughout the mansion.

“I am,” Ajax answered.

His father looked unconvinced.

“Then repeat what I said,” he demanded.

“You're going to be busy. Mother will be busy. The servants will be busier than both of you. And I'm supposed to take care of myself.”

A brief silence followed.

His mother sighed.

“Must you always make it sound so miserable?” she asked, guilt creeping into her voice.

“I believe that's the most family-friendly definition anyone could come up with for my situation right now,” Ajax retorted.

His father coughed.

It was a suspiciously well-timed cough, considering the amusement visible in his eyes.

Today was Ajax's little sister's birthday banquet. The entire mansion had been in a state of organised chaos since morning. Flowers were being arranged, silverware polished, decorations inspected, and an army of servants moved through the corridors carrying things Ajax dared not count.

“Your sister will be occupied with her guests,” his mother continued. “Your father and I will have to receive the representatives. There may be people asking to meet you as well.”

“I'll be fine,” Ajax said.

“Don't overexert yourself. And if you need anything, tell someone,” his father added.

Ajax nodded.

His mother adjusted the collar of his shirt, although it was already perfectly straight. His father placed a hand on his shoulder.

“Enjoy the evening, son.”

“Hmm…”

That appeared to satisfy them.

His parents exchanged one final glance before leaving. Within seconds, they were discussing the seating arrangements and the arrival of some particularly important guests.

Ajax watched them disappear down the corridor.

Then he looked around the mansion. He had approximately an hour before the guests started arriving.

An hour of freedom.

Not that he had anywhere particularly interesting to go.

*

The wheelchair moved with a quiet mechanical hum.

Ajax travelled through the eastern corridor, passing two servants carrying a large arrangement of white flowers.

“Those will look good in the main hall,” he remarked.

The older servant stopped.

“Yes, young master. We were just—”

"Take the tall sprays out first. The arrangement is too wide — it will not clear the doorframe otherwise."

The servant glanced at the flowers, then at the entrance.

“Oh. You're right.”

Ajax continued on his way.

A little later, he reached the front hall, where several servants were struggling to arrange the lighting. The wires running along the walls looked rather untidy.

Ajax approached and studied their work.

“Are the wires giving you trouble?”

The servant turned around.

“Ah! Yes, young master.”

“Why not hang curtains along both walls? You could run the wires behind them. That would hide the mess without interfering with the lights.”

Ajax pointed towards the walls as he explained.

The servant's expression gradually changed as he followed the suggestion.

“Thank you, young master!”

He bowed before hurrying off to put the idea into practice.

Ajax sighed and continued on his way.

He wasn't doing anything particularly impressive. He simply noticed the little things that others tended to overlook.

At least, more often than most people did.

He soon reached the balcony overlooking the western side of the mansion. His hands slowed, and then stopped moving the wheels.

Silver light fell across his face.

He turned his head and looked towards the sky.

Countless stars glittered above him, scattered across the darkness like a tapestry woven by the gods.

Ajax moved a little closer to the railing. A familiar fascination settled in his eyes.

He had once dreamed of exploring those skies.

Before the accident, he too had possessed an inextinguishable desire to reach those distant heights.

An accident had left his lower body completely paralysed. He could still vividly remember the pain—a kind of pain that made one forget how to scream.

At first, he thought he had adjusted. The difficulties of daily life were frustrating, certainly, but he had managed to make peace with his new circumstances.

It was the people around him who made that increasingly difficult.

His friends gradually stopped visiting. At first, he assumed they were busy. Soon, even their replies to his letters became less frequent.

At social gatherings, he began noticing the way people looked at him. Their gazes lingered for a moment before turning away, never returning.

Ajax had always been lively and optimistic, someone who enjoyed a good conversation. But as his old friendships faded, he began looking for other ways to occupy himself.

He read more. Studied more. Tried to become someone people would want to talk to.

Perhaps, he thought, if he became interesting enough, they would come back.

They didn't.

Three months ago, on his seventeenth birthday, he finally understood how far apart they had grown.

A few people still sent letters. Some of them were genuinely concerned, and he could feel it in their words. But there were so few of them.

The representatives came, too. They delivered an overly formal speech, congratulated him, and wished him a healthy life.

That was when Ajax finally accepted the reality he had spent so long trying to deny.

And that was when Ajax Wilhelmshire changed.

He slowly raised his left hand, gripping it tightly before extending it towards the sky. For a moment, he tried to grasp one of the stars.

Of course, he caught nothing.

It was an innocent gesture, perhaps. Or perhaps it was a yearning he had never managed to bury.

He wanted to ask the stars a question.

What had he done to deserve such a fate? What crime had earned him this punishment?

But the distance between them was too great for any answer to reach him.

Still, Ajax had made a decision long ago. Whatever the price, he would pay it if it meant he could stand again.

And when that day comes, he would demand an answer—even if he had to use force. It was an impossible dream according to worldly standards yet, Ajax held onto that imaginary like a child and refusing to let go.

He lowered his hand, turned his wheelchair, and hurried towards the main hall.

He passed the dining room, where several people were arranging the place settings.

“That's the wrong tablecloth.”

A young maid froze.

Ajax pointed towards the embroidered edge.

“The birthday girl's table is supposed to have the silver border. The gold ones belong to the dinner tables.”

“Oh! I apologise!”

The maid hurried to correct her mistake.

Ajax wasn't particularly fond of giving orders. He wasn't particularly fond of being treated like someone who needed constant supervision, either.

Fortunately, the servants had gradually learned that he was perfectly capable of noticing things.

Unfortunately, they had also learned that he noticed things they would rather he didn't.

*

By the time Ajax reached the main hall, the preparations were nearly complete.

The enormous chandelier overhead had been polished until it seemed almost like a second sun. Long tables stood along the sides of the room, decorated with flowers and carefully arranged silverware.

Servants moved between them, making the final adjustments.

And near the centre of the hall stood a boy.

He looked around Ajax's age, perhaps a little younger. He had brown hair, green eyes, and a rather ordinary face. He wore the uniform of the household staff, though his collar was slightly crooked.

The boy was standing in front of a decorative vase.

More precisely, he was rubbing it.

Ajax watched for a moment.

The boy rubbed the same spot.

Then again.

And again.

Ajax approached.

The boy didn't notice him. His attention was entirely occupied by the vase.

Ajax examined the object. It was a tall porcelain piece, decorated with blue patterns and a silver rim. Quite expensive, judging by the craftsmanship.

There was a small black mark on its side.

Ajax pointed towards it.

“There's still a spot there.”

The boy immediately looked down.

“Where?”

Ajax pointed again.

“Right there.”

“Oh.”

The boy leaned closer and rubbed the mark with his thumb.

Once. Then twice.

He applied a little more pressure. Even then, the black spot refused to budge.

Ajax watched silently.

The boy pulled out a cloth and began polishing the area.

A minute passed.

Ajax glanced at the nearby servants.

A few had begun watching.

Three minutes.

The boy changed the angle of his hand.

Five minutes.

He finally stopped.

The mark remained.

The boy stared at it.

Then at his hand.

Then back at the vase.

A strange silence had settled over the hall.

“Is it... still there?” he asked.

Ajax nodded.

The boy's expression became one of genuine despair.

He rubbed harder.

A servant nearby covered her mouth. Another turned away, his shoulders trembling.

The boy looked around.

Only then did he notice that nobody was working anymore.

Everyone was watching him.

He looked back at Ajax.

The young master sat in his wheelchair, his face perfectly composed.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then the boy's eyes widened.

“Wait.”

A servant made a strangled noise, desperately trying to contain his laughter.

Ajax smiled.

He had only meant to tease the boy, but seeing his expression, he couldn't help himself.

The boy stared at him with wounded dignity.

The servants finally burst into laughter.

“I'm sorry,” the boy said, though he sounded anything but. “I thought there was something wrong with the vase.”

“There was.”

“What?”

“A black spot.”

The boy looked ready to argue.

Ajax reached out and caught his hand.

“It's painted.”

The boy stared at him.

Then he looked at the vase once more.

The black mark was part of the decorative pattern.

He had spent five minutes polishing a painting.

The boy closed his eyes.

“Can we... keep it a secret from the outside world?”

Ajax laughed again.

He gave the boy's hand a friendly shake and lightly patted his back with the other.

“Good work.”

The boy looked at him.

“Thank you. I'll be sure to dedicate the next five minutes of my life to another artistic masterpiece.”

“Heh…”

Ajax chuckled.

For the first time in a long while, the evening didn't feel quite so dull.

And somewhere between their hands, Ajax felt a faint prickling sensation around his fingers.

It was light enough to be mistaken for a trick of the mind.

He glanced down at his left hand.

Nothing appeared unusual.

He ignored it.

“What's your name?” he asked.

The boy straightened his collar.

“It's Zhen... Zhen Shuri.”

“Ajax Wilhelmshire.”

“I know.”

“Then why didn't you say hello?”

Zhen looked at him.

“You were busy watching me polish a vase.”

Ajax considered that.

“Fair enough.”

Behind them, the servants were still laughing.
