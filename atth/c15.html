What the Guest Wants

1,693 words
Reading on

By the seventh day the province had a name for it.

They called it the quiet sickness, because it did not announce itself. There was no wasting, no lesions, no dramatic collapse. A man would feel a heaviness, then a pressure, and then — according to the physicians, who had begun to write reports in the flat language of people who had run out of explanations — a change in manner.

The reports used the word carefully. Not madness. Not possession. Manner.

One of the affected was a clerk in the eastern administration who had begun refusing to sign documents in his own hand. He would sit at his desk for hours, holding the pen, and when asked what he was waiting for, he said that he was waiting to be told which name to write.

Another was a woman who had stopped sleeping. Not from pain. She said that when she closed her eyes, she could feel what the others were doing.

There were forty-one reports of movement. There were now three hundred and six cases.

---

Ajax read all of it.

His father had begun bringing the reports up rather than bringing them to the communication chamber, because Ajax asked better questions than the clerks and because — though neither of them said it — the governor had begun to want his son's opinion.

On the seventh day Ajax asked for a map.

His father brought one. It was a survey of the province, marked with the affected districts in a careful hand, and Ajax spread it across the bed and looked at it for a long time without speaking.

"What do you see?" his father asked.

"I don't know yet." Ajax traced a line with one finger. "May I have the dates as well?"

"Dates?"

"When each case was reported. Not where. When."

His father looked at him.

"That is an odd thing to want."

"I know."

"May I ask why?"

Ajax did not look up from the map.

"Because if it is moving, it has a direction," he said. "And if it is spreading, it has a speed. And if I have a direction and a speed, I can work out where it was on the first day."

His father was silent for a moment.

"Which would tell us where it came from."

"Which would tell us where it came from," Ajax agreed.

---

The dates arrived that afternoon.

Ajax spent the rest of the day at the small table by the window, with the map weighted down by a water glass at each corner, and worked.

It was not difficult work. It was the same work he had done a hundred times with wiring and tablecloths and flower arrangements — the work of noticing that the world had a pattern and that nobody had bothered to draw it yet.

The first cases were at the palace and in a merchant house on the eastern road.

The second wave was in the districts between.

By the fourth day it had crossed the river.

He drew arcs. He drew arrows. He was not precise, and he knew he was not precise, and he wrote approximate three times in the margin so that he would not later mistake his own guess for a fact.

Then he sat back and looked at what he had drawn.

The centre of the arcs was not the palace.

The centre was north-east of the palace, past the outer town, in a district of warehouses and rented rooms and people who did not have physicians.

Ajax looked at that district for a while.

Then he called for his father.

---

"It started there," he said. "Not at the reception. The reception is where it was found."

Governor Wilhelmshire studied the map.

"The Crown has already investigated the warehouse district. They found nothing."

"They found nothing because they were looking for a contaminated shipment." Ajax tapped the centre of the arcs. "They should have been looking for a person."

---

The person was found the next morning.

She was a dock worker, forty-three years old, unmarried, no family in the city. She had been ill for eleven days — longer than anyone else — and had been treated for a chest infection by a physician who had not reported her because she had not attended the reception and therefore was not on any of the Crown's lists.

She had been coughing blood for nine days.

She had been going to work for seven of them.

---

The reports stopped using the word "manner" after that.

The Crown issued a second emergency order, and this one did not seal kitchens. It closed the docks, closed the eastern market, closed the roads out of the province, and authorised the Royal Medical Service to enter any household in which a person had been reported sleepless.

It also, in a clause that took three days to become public, authorised the Service to detain persons exhibiting changed manner pending examination.

Ajax read that clause twice.

Then he folded the paper and put it in his desk drawer and did not mention it to anyone.

---

His condition had not worsened, which the physicians found interesting and Ajax found alarming.

"You are not getting better," the younger physician said, on the ninth day. "But you are not getting worse, either. You are stable at a level of illness that should be progressing."

"Should be?"

"Every other patient is progressing." The physician looked at his notes rather than at Ajax. "Faster or slower, but always in the same direction. You have been at the same point for four days."

"Perhaps I am luckier than they are."

"Perhaps." The physician hesitated. "Or perhaps something is holding it still."

Ajax did not answer.

He had not told anyone what he had done that night with his own name. He had written it in his notebook and then written over it, and he had not tried it again, because he did not understand it and because he was afraid that trying it again would break whatever was holding.

But he thought about it constantly.

The self imposes its order.

He had read those words in a book with no title, in a dream that was not a dream, and had thought they were philosophy.

Now he was lying in a bed with something living behind his ribs, and the philosophy had done something.

He did not know what. He did not know whether it had helped or whether he had imagined it. But the thing inside him had stopped for one second when he held his own name up like a lamp, and the physicians were standing at his bedside saying you should be progressing, and Ajax Wilhelmshire had spent his whole life noticing things that other people overlooked.

He was not going to stop noticing now.

---

That night he tried again.

Not with his name. His name had stopped working on the second attempt, which he had half expected — it had been a thought, not a technique, and thoughts do not repeat.

He tried something smaller.

He thought about the garden. The orchid trees shedding petals onto the gravel. The specific way the light came through the eastern windows in the morning and made a stripe across the floorboards that he had watched every day for years without ever once deciding to.

He held that. Not the flower, not the idea of the garden — the actual stripe of light, on the actual floorboards, on a specific morning he could still see.

The thing behind his ribs shifted.

Not stopped. Shifted — a small, careful movement, like a man in a crowded room making space for someone to pass.

Ajax held the light-stripe and did not move and did not breathe.

And something that was not quite a thought, and not quite a voice, and not quite anything he had a word for, arrived in the back of his mind.

It was not language. It was closer to the way one knows the shape of a room before the lamp is lit. But if he had to translate it into words, the words would have been:

You are not empty.

Ajax lay very still.

You are not empty, the thing said again, or did not say, or simply was. That is unusual.

---

He did not answer it.

He did not know how to answer it. He did not know whether answering would be a mistake. He was seventeen years old and lying in a bed in his father's house with something that had killed three hundred people living behind his ribs, and it had just told him that he was not empty, and he had absolutely no idea what to do.

So he did what he had learned to do in a garden under the orchid trees, a month and a lifetime ago.

He observed.

He observed the thing observing him. He observed the network of Qi with its thin places and its thick places. He observed the way the thing moved toward the thin places and did not seem to know that he could see it doing so.

And he observed, with a small cold shock that he would remember for the rest of his life, that the thing was curious about him.

Not hungry. Not hostile. Curious.

You are not empty, it had said. That is unusual.

Ajax had spent his childhood being looked at with pity and his adolescence being looked at with discomfort. He had been the second young master, the one in the chair, the one people were kind to. Nobody had ever looked at him and found him unusual in a way that seemed to please them.

He thought, absurdly, that he might have liked it.

Then he thought that he was lying in bed having a feeling about a plague, and that this was exactly the sort of thing that got a man detained under the new clause.

He closed his eyes.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow I will think about this properly.

The thing behind his ribs did not move again that night.

It was, Ajax would later reflect, the first night since the fever began that he slept well.
