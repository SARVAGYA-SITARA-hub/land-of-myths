Against the Heavens · Chapter 6

Learning to Feel

2,852 words
Reading on

The garden was quiet in the early morning.

Ajax sat beneath the shade of an orchid tree, his wheelchair positioned beside a small stone table. Beyond the garden, the road in front of the Wilhelmshire estate stretched towards the distant city. Rows of orchid trees stood along the outer wall, their branches covered in clusters of pink blossoms.

A few petals drifted through the air.

Cars passed along the road at regular intervals. Inside the estate, servants moved between the flowerbeds, trimming branches, watering plants and carrying out the ordinary duties of the morning.

Ajax watched them for a while.

A servant stood a short distance away, waiting for instructions.

"That will be all for now."

"Would young master like anything else?"

"No. You may attend to your other duties. I'll call if I need something."

The servant bowed and left.

Ajax reached for the water bottle on the table. Its curved surface contained a small crystal, held in place by a metal setting. Several runes had been inscribed around it, allowing the bottle to maintain the temperature of its contents.

The water was still warm.

He took a sip and placed it back.

Then he closed his eyes.

The instructions from Cheonma's diary returned to his mind.

Stillness of the body.

Stillness of the senses.

Observe the world.

Ajax settled into a comfortable position and began to regulate his breathing. He had some familiarity with Pranayama, though he had never practised it with any particular seriousness. This time, however, he paid attention to each breath, allowing it to arrive and leave without forcing it.

He listened.

The sound of passing cars.

The rustling of leaves.

The distant footsteps of servants.

A bird calling somewhere beyond the wall.

He tried to notice the intervals between sounds, the differences in their distance, and the subtle changes that occurred as the wind moved through the garden.

Half an hour passed.

Ajax opened his eyes.

Nothing.

He looked at his hands, then at the garden.

There was no strange sensation, no sudden clarity, and certainly no mysterious energy flowing through his body.

He felt exactly as he had before.

"...Well."

He picked up the water bottle.

"That was rather uneventful."

He took another sip.

It was not particularly disappointing. The instructions had never promised immediate results. In fact, the diary had repeatedly warned against impatience.

Still, he had expected something.

Perhaps not a dramatic transformation, but at least a small indication that he was doing something correctly.

Instead, he had spent half an hour sitting beneath a tree.

Ajax considered the matter for a moment.

Then he decided to try again tomorrow.

There was no reason to abandon something simply because it had failed once.

*

The following morning, Ajax asked one of the estate's attendants to purchase a diary for him.

It was a small, dark-covered book with runes inscribed along its edges. The runes were designed to recognise their owner and prevent anyone else from opening its pages.

A rather convenient feature.

He examined it briefly before placing it beside his bed.

That evening, he began writing.

He recorded everything he could remember from Cheonma's diary.

The exercises.

The warnings.

The passages about the heavens and the nature of transcendence.

He even copied down a few sentences that had particularly caught his attention.

The first step is not to command Qi. It is to recognise that it exists.

Below it, he added his own observation.

I have yet to recognise anything.

He paused.

Then wrote another line.

Perhaps I am simply doing it incorrectly.

The next morning, he returned to the garden.

And the morning after that.

For seven days, Ajax repeated the exercises.

He sat beneath the orchid trees, closed his eyes, regulated his breathing and observed the world around him. Sometimes he practised in the garden. On other days, he remained in his room, listening to the sounds beyond his window.

He recorded the duration of each attempt and anything that seemed unusual.

There was very little to record.

His breathing became more comfortable. He found that he could remain still for longer periods without becoming distracted. He also began noticing sounds he would ordinarily ignore.

But Qi?

Nothing.

He was beginning to suspect that his greatest achievement so far was becoming better at sitting quietly.

It was a rather modest accomplishment.

*

During the same week, Ajax returned to the library.

The family library was extensive, containing historical records, old geographical surveys, accounts of ancient civilisations and several collections of translated manuscripts.

He had originally intended to spend his remaining time studying the Central Academy's preliminary curriculum.

That plan had not been abandoned.

It had merely acquired a new competitor.

Cheonma.

Ajax searched through several historical collections, looking for the name and the strange writing he remembered from the dream.

The name was difficult to trace.

Different books used different spellings, and some old records appeared to describe Cheonma as a title rather than an individual name. The writing in the diary was unfamiliar, but a few characters resembled those found in certain older manuscripts.

He made notes of the similarities.

Nothing conclusive emerged.

There were references to an ancient figure associated with martial philosophy, but the accounts were fragmented. Some described a wandering ascetic. Others spoke of a man who had rejected the authority of the heavens.

Ajax found the contradictions more interesting than the stories themselves.

If these accounts referred to the same person, then either the historians knew very little about him or the man had been considerably more complicated than the legends suggested.

He copied the relevant passages into his private diary.

One particular account caught his attention.

According to an old record, Cheonma had once withdrawn into the wilderness to understand the nature of Qi. He had spent three months observing the natural world before he managed to recognise it.

Three months.

Ajax read the passage twice.

Then he leaned back.

"...That's reassuring."

He had spent a week without accomplishing anything.

Apparently, the person who had written the instructions had needed considerably longer.

It was difficult to tell whether this made the task easier or more discouraging.

Ajax decided it was the former.

At least he was not falling behind.

*

The eighth morning began like the others.

Ajax sat beneath the orchid tree, his private diary resting on the table beside him.

He had repeated the exercises several times, but the results remained almost identical.

He could notice the wind.

He could distinguish the footsteps of a servant from those of someone walking along the road.

He could recognise the changes in his own breathing.

But none of these things brought him any closer to feeling Qi.

He opened his diary and read through his notes.

There were several pages of observations, followed by a few questions.

If Qi exists throughout the natural world, why can it not be perceived through ordinary observation?

Does it require a particular state of the body?

Is the inability to perceive it a limitation of the senses, or a misunderstanding of what is being perceived?

He tapped his pen against the page.

The final question bothered him.

Perhaps he had been approaching the matter from the wrong direction.

Cheonma's instructions were clear enough. Observe the world. Do not force sensations. Do not imagine Qi and mistake the imagination for discovery.

But the diary was not a collection of unquestionable truths.

It was a record of someone's journey.

There were sections in which Cheonma presented questions without answers. Other passages contained theories, possibilities and observations that seemed to contradict one another. Some ideas were written with certainty, while others were followed by notes expressing doubt.

The man had not simply written down what he knew.

He had also written down what he did not know.

Ajax found that rather reassuring.

If Cheonma had already understood everything, there would have been little reason to keep a diary.

He looked towards the small collection of discarded magical devices that had been brought to the garden for inspection and disposal. A few of them contained small energy crystals. They were no longer useful for their original purposes, but the crystals themselves appeared intact.

Ajax's gaze settled on them.

An idea occurred to him.

It was not particularly elegant.

In fact, it went against the very spirit of the first lesson.

But he considered it anyway.

If Qi existed throughout the world, and the energy stored within crystals was a form of it, then perhaps he could use one as a reference.

He had spent a week trying to recognise something he could not perceive.

What if he first experienced it in a form that was impossible to overlook?

Ajax examined the nearest device.

The crystal inside was small, no larger than the tip of his thumb. It was an ordinary storage crystal, the kind used in several household appliances.

He removed it from the discarded device.

Then he hesitated.

The diary had warned him against forcing sensations.

He looked at the crystal.

"...This is probably a bad idea."

He placed it on the table.

A few moments passed.

Then he picked it up again.

He was not entirely convinced that the experiment would work. Nor was he certain that it was a good idea. But he had spent seven days repeating the same exercise without making progress.

Perhaps a different approach was worth considering.

He placed the crystal over a prepared receptacle and carefully broke its casing.

The stored energy escaped.

For an instant, a faint glow spread across the surface.

Then the energy rushed outward.

Ajax felt it.

It was like an electric current passing through him, except that the sensation was not entirely physical. Something unfamiliar brushed against his senses, spreading through his awareness before he could properly understand it.

His fingers tightened.

The sensation disappeared almost as quickly as it had arrived.

Ajax remained still.

His heart was beating faster.

He looked at the broken crystal.

Then at his hands.

"...That was something."

He took a moment to settle himself before writing anything.

He did not want to confuse surprise with understanding.

The sensation had been brief, but it was distinct. It had not felt like warmth, cold, pressure or pain. It was something else.

He opened his diary.

Experiment: Exposure to stored energy crystal.

Observation: Upon release, the energy produced a sensation distinguishable from ordinary physical sensations.

He paused.

Then added:

Possible relation to Qi.

He underlined the final words.

It was not proof.

But it was the first time he had felt something that might be Qi.

*

The following days were considerably more productive.

Ajax did not repeat the experiment carelessly. He began by observing the crystals before their energy was released, noting their faint glow and the subtle changes that occurred when they were used in ordinary devices.

He also paid attention to the sensations left behind after the first experiment.

At first, he could only recognise the energy when it was released suddenly.

Gradually, however, he began noticing something else.

The energy did not seem to disappear completely.

There was a faint impression left in the surroundings, almost like the lingering presence of a sound after it had stopped.

He could not tell whether this was genuinely Qi or merely the result of his senses remembering the previous experience.

So he recorded both possibilities.

Some of his notes became increasingly speculative.

Perhaps perception is not the same as sensation.

Perhaps the body does not need to feel Qi in the same way it feels heat or pressure.

If the world is filled with Qi, then the inability to perceive it may not mean that it is absent.

Perhaps the observer is the problem.

He stopped after writing the last sentence.

Then he added:

This is only a hypothesis.

He had no intention of pretending otherwise.

The recurring dreams continued as well.

Every time Ajax fell asleep, he returned to the endless expanse beneath the stars.

The stone table remained.

The brown diary remained.

And Cheonma's writing continued to reveal itself, section by section.

Some nights, the diary offered more practical instructions. On others, it contained philosophical discussions that seemed to have little connection to cultivation.

There were passages about the relationship between the self and the heavens, the nature of will, and the difference between possessing power and having the right to use it.

Some sections ended with questions.

Others contained incomplete theories.

Ajax began to understand that the diary was not necessarily presenting a finished path.

It was presenting the thoughts of someone who had walked one.

And perhaps, in some places, even Cheonma had been uncertain.

That possibility changed how Ajax read it.

He stopped treating every sentence as an instruction.

Instead, he began asking why it had been written.

*

Two weeks passed.

By then, Ajax had become more familiar with the sensation produced by the crystals.

He could recognise it more readily, though he still could not properly explain what he was feeling.

The distinction between external energy and the faint impressions he had begun noticing around him became clearer.

He returned to the original exercises.

Stillness of the body.

Stillness of the senses.

Observation of the world.

This time, he approached them differently.

He no longer expected the exercises to produce a sudden result. He simply tried to recognise what was already present.

The garden became his usual place of practice.

He would sit beneath the orchid tree, listening to the road and watching the movement of the leaves. Occasionally, he would look at the warm-water bottle on the table, paying attention to the crystal embedded in its surface.

The device had become an unexpectedly useful object of study.

It contained stored energy, and the runes around it regulated the flow to maintain the water's temperature.

He could not understand the entire mechanism, but he could observe the relationship between the crystal, the runes and the changes in the water.

One morning, while repeating the exercises, he noticed something.

It was faint.

So faint that he almost dismissed it.

But there was a difference between the sensation he remembered from the released crystals and the feeling he was now beginning to recognise.

The former had been sudden and overwhelming.

This was quieter.

It seemed to exist beneath the ordinary sensations of his body, almost hidden behind them.

Ajax stopped moving.

He did not try to grasp it.

He did not attempt to direct it.

He simply observed.

For several moments, the sensation remained.

Then it faded.

Ajax opened his eyes.

His gaze fell upon the diary.

He reached for it and turned to a clean page.

Observation: There appears to be a distinction between the energy perceived outside the body and the energy present within it.

He paused.

The former is easier to recognise when stimulated. The latter is considerably more subtle.

He read the sentences again.

Then he added another line.

I do not yet understand whether they are fundamentally different or merely different expressions of the same thing.

He set the pen down.

For the first time, he felt that he had made some genuine progress.

Not a breakthrough.

Not mastery.

Just progress.

*

That evening, Ajax returned to his room earlier than usual.

His private diary lay open on the table. Several pages were filled with observations, questions and small diagrams.

Beside it was the academy prospectus.

He had nearly forgotten about it.

He glanced at the remaining course descriptions before closing the booklet.

There was still much to prepare before leaving for the Central Academy. He would need to decide which books to take, arrange his personal belongings and make sure the necessary accommodations were prepared.

And, apparently, he would need to continue studying an ancient man's rather unconventional philosophy.

Ajax looked at the diary.

He had initially considered the recurring dream an elaborate trick of his mind.

But dreams did not usually repeat themselves with such consistency. Nor did they provide new information each time.

He still had no proof that Cheonma had existed.

He had no proof that the diary was anything more than a strange inheritance.

And he certainly had no proof that the path described within it would lead anywhere.

Yet he had begun to feel something.

That was difficult to dismiss.

He opened his private diary one last time and wrote:

Cheonma spent three months attempting to perceive Qi.

I have spent three weeks.

I cannot say that I understand his methods, nor can I confirm that my interpretation is correct.

However, I have begun to distinguish something that I could not perceive before.

He considered the page.

Then, beneath it, he wrote:

Perhaps the purpose of this diary is not to provide answers.

Perhaps it is to teach me how to ask better questions.

Ajax closed the book.

Outside, the evening wind moved through the orchid trees. A few pink petals drifted past his window.

He watched them for a while.

Then he turned towards his bed.

Tomorrow, he would try again.

And perhaps, eventually, he would understand what Cheonma had been trying to show him.

For now, learning to notice was enough.
