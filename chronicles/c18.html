Interview - IV

2,496 words

Arin stepped back into the room. His face was still pale, but the set of his shoulders had changed. Slightly looser, as if he had exhaled something he'd been holding since the beginning of the test. .

He sat. This time, his hands did not clamp down immediately on his thighs. They rested there, fingers interlaced, knuckles not quite as white.

"Apologies for the interruption, sir, ma'am," he said. His voice was steadier than when he had left. "Thank you for the minute."

Sen inclined her head. "Are you able to continue?"

"Yes, ma'am," he said. There was less performance in it now.

Orlov watched him in silence for a long heartbeat.

"Very well," Orlov said at last. "Let us try again."

He leaned forward, forearms on the table, the old soldier coming back to the surface.

"Forget merit. Forget narrative. Forget whatever your coaching manuals told you we want to hear." His gaze held Arin's, steady and unblinking. "Tell us about a time you changed your mind. One specific moment. No theory. No slogans. Just you."

Beside him, Sen clicked her pen open, ready. Hayato's fingers tightened slightly around his folded hands, the only sign of anticipation he allowed himself.

He drew in a breath, slow enough that the Frame could catch and steady it around his left lung.

"Seven months ago," he said, "a doctor told my mother I had about a year."

Instructor Sen's pen paused. Mr. Hayato's gaze flicked down, checking the file, then back up to his face. Orlov did not move at all.

"He told her in the next room," Arin said. "I heard it through the wall. And then I tasted it, later, when she made me drink my medicine." A small, wry curve touched the corner of his mouth. "Bitter herbs. Cheap tablets. Chalky aftertaste that sticks to your tongue like dust. You can't swallow it all the way down. It just sits there, reminding you."

"At that time, I believed two things very strongly," he said. "One, that I was going to die soon. Two, that the world owed me a little softness on the way out."

Instructor Sen's eyes shifted, almost imperceptibly, to the medical note in his file, as if re-reading the prognosis that sat there like an old bruise.

"My mother didn't cry in front of me," Arin continued. "She sat by the bed. Checked my temperature. Went to the kitchen. Came back. The only place she let anything escape was—" he hesitated, searching for the right words "—her shoulders, when she thought I was asleep. They dropped for two seconds. Then she put them back."

He smiled, small and genuine, as if the memory hurt and warmed him at the same time.

"A few days later," he said, "she came home with a book."

"A book?," Mr. Hayato murmured.

"Yes, sir," Arin said. "Institute of Inter World Studies General Track Foundation Practice Compendium, second edition. Tape on the spine, pages missing at the back, someone else's notes in the margins. She put it on the table like it was something fragile."

He took another breath.

"Fifty rupees," he said quietly. "That's what she paid. I did the math. That's… medicine we didn't buy. Groceries we didn't buy. She won't say it that way, but I've seen her negotiate with vegetable sellers. She doesn't throw money. If she spent fifty on a broken book, it means she cut something else."

Sen's fingers tightened around the pen. For the first time, there was a faint tension at the line of her jaw.

"I remember thinking," Arin said, "'This is unfair.' Not the book. Everything. That there are families who never have to choose between dal and textbooks, and mine does, and now on top of it I have an expiry date." The admission came out almost light.

"Then I started studying," he went on. "Partly because I was angry. Partly because there was nothing else to do in a twelve-by-ten room except count cracks in the ceiling. But also because—" He exhaled. "Because if she could cut groceries for me, the least I could do was not waste the pages."

He lifted his gaze fully to them now.

"That was the first shift," he said. "From 'The world owes me' to 'I owe this person in front of me.'"

He let that sit for a heartbeat, then added, almost conversationally:

"And then I realized she wasn't the only one."

Mr. Hayato's head tilted a fraction.

"When I used to traverse between library in a bus," Arin said, choosing the word carefully, "I saw a lot of people trying to fight their way out of small rooms. There was a boy whose mother worked as domestic help. She couldn't promise him coaching, or a college seat. All she could give him was a place to sleep between shifts."

He swallowed, the movement visible at his throat.

He looked down, and when he looked up again there was that same quiet, genuine smile the panel had not seen on him before.

"I remember thinking, 'I'm not even the most unfortunate person in this line,'" he said. "My mother could still buy me a book, something in my head… turned."

He spread his fingers slightly on his knees, as if putting the shape of it into the air.

"I stopped asking, 'Why is this happening to me?'" he said. "I started asking, 'If this is happening anyway, what can I make out of it that isn't just bitterness?'"

Silence settled again, denser now. Even the air-conditioning hum seemed to recede.

"So when you ask, sir," Arin finished, looking at Orlov, "about a time I changed my mind… I think it was then. When I stopped treating my life as something the world was doing to me, and started treating it as something I'm responsible for, even if the parameters are terrible."

There was no flourish at the end. No manifesto. Just a boy sitting very straight in a too-big chair.

Orlov's face did not change, but Hayato's fingers tightened slightly where they were folded. Instructor Sen had forgotten, for a few seconds, to take notes.

The genuine smile lingered at the corners of Arin's mouth, almost apologetic and yet utterly unperformed.

Orlov broke the silence.

"And you think," he said slowly, "that telling us about dying and tiffin boxes will help you bargain with entities that see our planet as a resource node?"

The question landed like a lead weight.

Arin shook his head once.

"No, sir," he said. "I don't think anyone on the other side of that fog is interested in my mother's grocery budget."

His tone remained calm, almost gentle.

"But those experiences are important for me," he added, "to become the person who sits at that table and doesn't forget what he's representing."

Instructor Sen's eyes narrowed, just a fraction.

"Explain," she said.

"Diplomacy is not about making everyone happy," Arin said. "It's an art of negotiation where both sides can go home and keep face in front of their people while getting something that actually matters."

He glanced briefly at Mr. Hayato, then back.

"If I'm sitting in a room with a foreign civilization," he continued, "what matters on paper is tonnage, energy units, security guarantees. But what decides whether a treaty survives the first crisis is something smaller and messier—the emotions of ordinary people who will live under that agreement."

He lifted one hand slightly, then let it rest again.

"I'm… glad," he said, "that I've finally learned how those feel from the inside. Powerlessness. Humiliation. Gratitude when someone offers even a tiny bit of dignity. I don't think that makes me a better negotiator overnight. But I think it makes me less likely to treat people as numbers on a briefing slide."

Hayato's expression shifted, almost imperceptibly, into something like approval.

"Knowledge of such emotions," Arin finished, "isn't a bargaining chip I throw on the table. It's… the shape of the person doing the bargaining."

Orlov's gaze had gone very still.

"How would that shape your choices?" he asked. "Give me something concrete, not philosophy."

Arin nodded.

"If a civilization comes to the table clearly holding the lower hand," he said, "The most safe answer would be, 'Good. Press the advantage. Snatch as much as possible while you can.'"

Instructor Sen knew that was true; she had heard versions of it in mock interviews often enough.

"I'd do something different," Arin said.

"Go on," Orlov said.

"I'd push for terms that are still in our favour—our duty is to protect our people first—but I'd build in pockets of leniency where their delegation can save face," Arin replied. "Concessions that don't break our security, but matter symbolically for them."

"Symbolically?" Sen repeated.

"Maybe it's the way the agreement is worded," he said. "Maybe it's a shared project we label as cooperation instead of aid. Something that lets them go home and say, 'We didn't crawl,' even if everyone in the room knows who had leverage."

He met Orlov's eyes.

"That way, they owe us twice," he added quietly. "Once for the material terms. Once for the fact that we didn't humiliate them when we could have. That second debt… becomes a favour you can call in later, when the tables have turned or when you need a partner instead of a subject."

He did not sound triumphant. If anything, he sounded faintly tired.

"I've been on the other side of that feeling," he said. "Of someone with power choosing not to grind my face into the floor just because they could. You don't forget it. You think of them differently after."

For the first time since the interview began, Orlov's eyes warmed by a degree.

"And if your leniency makes them greedy?" he asked, voice softening in tone but hardening in content. "If they look at your kindness and say, 'These humans are weak; why negotiate at all when we can demand more?'"

The air in the room shifted.

It was not visible. There was no crackle of energy, no glow. But all at once the space between the table and Arin's chair thickened, as if someone had quietly increased the gravity in that narrow passage

The Archive flickered at the edge of his vision.

[External hostile aura detected.]

[Classification tentative High tier combatant.]

His lungs stuttered. The Null Frame around his left lobe flickered, strained; for an instant, his breath scraped like it had in the days before he learned to build it.

[Heart Rate +42 above baseline.]

[Blood pressure spike. Muscular tremor imminent.]

Across the table, Instructor Sen's spine snapped straighter. Her hand tightened on the pen.

"Chairman Orlov," she began, a warning coiled in his name.

Mr. Hayato's pupils had narrowed, the only sign that he, too, could feel the invisible weight pressing down on the boy. His fingers shifted under the table, the faintest hint of a warding gesture never completed.

But Orlov did not look at them. His attention was fixed entirely on Arin.

"What if," he said again, voice low, "the other side looks at your mercy and decides there is no point in negotiating? That you are soft. That they should squeeze harder. Demand more resources. More access. What then, boy?"

His body reacted before his mind could.

His knees buckled. The chair scraped; he slipped forward, catching himself too late. He hit the floor on one knee, palm slamming down to stop himself from collapsing completely. The world narrowed to the space between his head and the table's edge.

[Frame collapse warning..], the Archive blared.

His hand shook against the carpet. His lungs burned; black edged his vision, creeping in like spilled ink.

"Enough," Instructor Sen snapped, the word sharp. "Chairman—"

"Orlov-san," Hayato said at the same time, voice still mild but threaded with steel.

Neither of them had time to rise.

Because Arin, trembling, did something that made all three of them stop.

He looked up.

Not at the floor, not at the nearest exit. At Orlov. Straight into the older man's eyes.

His own eyes were wide, pupils blown from strain. His face had gone several shades paler. But the line of his gaze did not break.

[Unbroken Will], the Archive noted distantly, to itself. [Field test: ongoing.]

Very slowly, muscles shaking, Arin dragged his back foot under him. His free hand found the edge of the chair.

The invisible weight pushed back.

His chest screamed; his lungs felt like paper being crumpled in a giant fist. But the Null Frame caught, flickered, caught again—a stuttering scaffolding around failing tissue that should not have been able to bear this much pressure.

He rose anyway.

First to a crouch, then to a half-stand, then finally upright, one hand braced on the chair, the other still hanging at his side. His legs shook. His breaths came short and ugly. But he was on his feet.

Sen's pen had stopped moving entirely. Hayato's brows had drawn together, the faintest shock cracking his composure.

Orlov's killing intent did not vanish, but it pulled back a fraction, turning from crushing weight to heavy presence. Enough to let the boy breathe. Not enough to erase what had just happened.

Arin swallowed once. His throat worked around air that felt like it had to fight for every inch. He did not wipe the sweat gathering at his temple. He simply stood there, looking at the man who had just tried to fold him.

"When they demand more?" he said, voice hoarse but steady. "When they decide there's no point in talking because they think I'm weak?"

He straightened another millimetre, as if the sentence itself were a brace.

"Then I'd teach them," Arin continued, each word clear, "I am not here to beg."

The sentence dropped into the space between them like a marker placed firmly on a map.

Not shouted. Just stated, quietly, by someone whose body was visibly losing a battle his will refused to concede.

For the first time, something like surprise broke cleanly across Orlov's face. Not at the words—he had heard variations of defiance before—but at the fact that they were coming from a boy whose lungs should have folded long before his knees did, and who had stood up under his killing intent where even veteran soldiers faltered.

The killing intent withdrew completely, like a tide going out. The room's pressure normalized so suddenly that Sen had to stop herself from exhaling aloud. Mr. Hayato's shoulders eased by a fraction.

Arin did not sit immediately. He held Orlov's gaze for one more heartbeat, making sure the words had landed where they were meant to.
