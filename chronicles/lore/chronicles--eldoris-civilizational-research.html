Part 1 — The cosmology, now that it's fixed

1.1 The chain of descent

   │

SPIRIT KINGS  (Tarkhan — Dispersion; Panak — earth)
   │
   ▼
THE BLESSED — ordinary glow-coloured fighters

The fifth piece is Aezion's actual power, intact, hidden somewhere. Full account: The Shattering.

ESTABLISHED on the page: glow colour marks a blessing. Ch 41 lists "red, blue, green and brown" as the common glows a fighter will have seen; a white-grey glow is rare enough to be mistaken for a Spirit King's. Ch 54 gives Panak a brown glow and Vazir an orange one. Ch 57 explains Tarkhan's as "the blessing of the Spirit King of Water, and the quality of it was Dispersion."

Five rungs, not three: blessed → Spirit King → element spirit → great spirit → Aezion.

1.2 What "Noor-e-Zaya" tells us

A shopkeeper in Tilmis kneels mid-transaction and murmurs "I bow for thy blessings, our lord and savior Noor-e-Zaya" (ch 46). He is not praying to Aezion. He is praying to an element spirit — one of the jewels' children.

PROPOSED — the most important structural implication:

Every blessing on the continent descends from Aezion consolidated into one jewel, broken into five when the priest's hymns were smeared by rain. So every blessing is a fragment of the creator. A blessed man carries a piece of the god who made the world, and does not know it.

That makes the power system a form of unwitting relic-worship, and makes Azevalgar's strategy obvious and terrifying: he does not need to defeat an army. He needs to collect or corrupt the fragments. Every blessing taken is a piece of Aezion removed from the board.

It also means the spirits are not gods. They are debris — and people pray to debris because the original is not there any more. That is exactly why Parshara "does not remember," and why a border guard can sneer at an old god.

1.3 The four jewels and Nazarana's four cities

PROPOSED — the strongest single idea in this document.

Nazarana is governed from four cities over four branches of business. The creator shattered into four great spirits, which became four jewels.

CityBranchProposed jewel / element
Tilmis (north)scholarship, post, the World History Treethe record — what is remembered
Ganj (east)ores, minerals, appraisalearth — what is weighed
Baazar (west)the largest market— the one that circulates
Somalis (south)information, mercenaries— what is known

Now read what your vault already states, with new eyes:

"Kalman's Oath when broken will curse that lineage forever and no one from that lineage shall receive the blessings of spirits for years to come."

Why would breaking an oath cost a lineage its spirit blessings — unless the spirits themselves are the oath-keepers?

PROPOSED: Kalman's Oath is sworn on the four jewels, and enforced by the four great spirits, not by law. It is not a legal fiction with a curse attached. It is a contract with held collateral: the jewels physically rest in the four cities, and a broken oath means the spirits withdraw.

Three consequences, all free:

  1. The governors' private armies have a purpose. They are not vanity. Each garrisons the jewel in their own city, so no three can move against the fourth without losing a spirit.
  2. The oath is breakable — by seizing a jewel. Not by lawyering it. That is a heist, not a coup, and a heist is a better plot.
  3. Azevalgar's route into the continent is the jewels. He does not need an army to destroy Eldoris; he needs four thefts. And the conspiracy that approached the Nazarana crown (ch 48) was offering precisely the help that would get him close.

It also explains why the crown has no army of its own — it does not need one. It needs the jewels to stay where they are. The crown's entire power is the continued existence of four objects. That is a magnificent amount of fragility for a state.

1.4 The seal is already broken, and the text says so

Ch 46, in Tilmis, the city of records:

"The statue was tall, its surface webbed with faint cracks that betrayed its age. The stone had lost its luster, but the figure still carried an air of majesty. The plaque at its base read: [ Protector of the Land of Kuru ] Lord Azevalgar"

Azevalgar — the outer god who wants to destroy the continent — is the protector deity of Nazarana's founding oath, and the one the oath is "overseen by."

Your vault says it plainly: "The Kalman's oath is said to be overseen by Azevalgar (Protector of Kuru). Even the pale men seem to know about Kalman's oath."

This is the best thing in your worldbuilding and I do not think it has been used yet.

Nazarana's entire constitutional order — the thing that stops four merchant armies eating each other — is guaranteed by the enemy of the world. The oath that keeps Kuru intact is administered by the god trying to end Eldoris.

Which means:

  • The oath has never been a protection. It is a leash, and Nazarana has held the wrong end of it for six hundred years.
  • "No one from that lineage shall receive the blessings of spirits" is not a punishment Azevalgar invented. It is what Azevalgar does. He takes fragments of Aezion. That is the mechanism.
  • The pale men knowing about Kalman's Oath is not trivia. They are his agents, and they know the instrument because their master wrote it.
  • His statue stands in Tilmis, the city that records everything, and nobody questions it. The most important fact in the setting is a public monument in the most literate city on the continent, and the population walks past it.

One line of dialogue fixes the arc's stakes: someone asks why the Protector of Kuru is a cracked, lustreless statue — and the answer is that it was never a statue of a protector. It is a receipt.

1.5 The exemption — confirmed

ESTABLISHED (author). The oath binds the crown, the four governors and their lineages. It does not bind the Order of Zenos, because Azevalgar created the Order.

So Zenos can betray a governor, or the crown, with no backlash whatsoever — no curse, no lost blessings, nothing.

That makes the four governors' position exactly as bad as it looks:

They are protected fromThey are exposed to
Each otherZenos — completely and costlessly
The crown—

The oath froze the four houses in place for six hundred years, and then a party that exists outside the oath was given the freedom of the board. They are locked in a cage with a guard who works for the escapee.

Read Kalman's Oath for the full mechanic, including the two consequences the prose should carry: a governor can be punished for trying to leave but never for what he does, and the only defection available is joining the organisation that is hunting him.

Part 3 — The civilizations, with analogues

3.1 Nazarana — the Land of Kuru

ESTABLISHED. Four cities, four governors, four branches of business, no nobles, private armies, Kalman's Oath, moustaches as male fashion, silk over-garment with a breast emblem and buttoned ankle trousers, forests north before Somalis, farms mainly in the east.

In the text (ch 46, Tilmis): golden gates, smooth white stone walls, roads that look "carved from a single long stone," lamp posts rather than glowing stones, monuments in many languages, checkpoints, a giant wheel, "children with wide smiles and plump bodies." The post office is enormous, "numerous counters lined the walls, each attended by a different clerk," and "the interior felt strangely cold, the opposite of the heat outside."

The governing line: Arin sees the plump children and "the faces of thin, hollow-cheeked servants flashed in his mind."

ESTABLISHED (ch 54) — the four governors, named:

GovernorCityGlow

Silvaris DonnaGanj—

They attend council by projection, not in person — "With that, Silvaris's projection vanished." Vazir's own aura is orange.

This corrects v1. I proposed a physical meeting hall for the four governors. The text already has remote projection, which is better: every council scene has a body count of ghosts, and Vazir is alone in a room with four armed men who are not there. Keep it.

It also sharpens his line: "Everywhere I go in this castle, I can feel their gazes." He cannot tell which shadows are projections.

Analogue: the Hanseatic League

NazaranaHanseatic League
Four governors of four citiesFour quarters: Wendish, Saxon, Prussian-Livonian, Westphalian

Why the League failed, and what it means for you: no central treasury, no navy, no way to compel a member that refused. It did not lose a war — it became obsolete.

PROPOSED flaw: Nazarana's crown has no army and no tax. Its only power is an oath guaranteed by a hostile god. The state therefore ends the moment someone proves the oath is void — and per §1.4, someone already has, because Azevalgar's statue is cracked and lustreless in the open.

**What to borrow — the waqf. Ottoman bazaar buildings were religious endowments; rents funded hospices and schools, and the property could not be sold, seized, or taxed. Make Baazar's market sacred property** and Baazar becomes the continent's neutral ground by law rather than arms — and it gains a missing institution: a clerical class of accountants who administer the endowment. A market run by priests of the ledger is very nasty and fits a book whose cosmology is bureaucratic.

**What to borrow — the barīd.** The Islamic postal service was also the intelligence service; postmasters doubled as informants and reported prices, trials, weather and the conduct of officials to the capital.

You have already split this in two: Tilmis owns the post, Somalis owns information. Those are the two halves of one organ, in two rival cities, neither able to work without the other, both reading the other's mail, and the crown dependent on both. An espionage triangle that costs one sentence to establish.

What to borrow — Timbuktu. A desert-edge caravan stop that became a scholarly capital through private family libraries rather than a state archive, with books as a trade good. Gives Tilmis a concrete economy: it does not own knowledge, it owns copies, and the copying is the business.

What to borrow — the Sogdians. A trading diaspora whose language became the continent's lingua franca, with colonies in every foreign city. Gives Somalis a reason to exist beyond thuggery: they are everywhere, they speak everything, and the information network is the by-product of being the only people who can talk to both sides.

3.2 Parshara

ESTABLISHED. Under Nazarana's sanctions. Has a Royal Messenger and a Royal Scholar (Arin and Hayato's assumed posts). Insignia checked at borders. Its people starve — "My daughter in law is only able to eat once in two days." A Hearthglen guard sneers: "Aren't those the ones who still worship that old god?"

Two new facts from the prose, both large:

1. Marine concrete. "Made up when a certain ratio of sea metals are mixed along with infusing the energy of sea. Originating in Parshara kingdom this material thickens overtime when comes in contact with water." The castle walls Arin sees have "visible pores" — that is the material.

2. Glowing stones. Ch 54, Vazir to the council: "I want to resume importing glowing stones from the Parshara Kingdom, given the mounting cost of oil and other fuels."

This changes the sanctions completely. Parshara is not merely a food producer under blockade. Parshara is the continent's energy supplier. Glowing stones are a fuel, Nazarana has been cut off, and the council's objection is economic before it is political. Nazarana lights its streets with lamp posts because the stones stopped coming.

So the blockade is not "keep the enemy poor." It is "deny the enemy the market for the thing we cannot do without, so we can buy it cheaply later" — and the four governors profit from the shortage. A real sanctions dynamic, and it makes the war legible.

Analogue: Roman marine concrete + Zoroastrian fire

The concrete is Roman, not Persian. Opus caementicium — pozzolanic concrete with volcanic ash — sets and strengthens in seawater and self-heals cracks through growth of aluminium tobermorite and phillipsite. It is why Roman harbours outlived modern piers by two thousand years.

PROPOSED political consequence: if Parshara's concrete strengthens in water and repairs itself, Parshara's harbours cannot be besieged. Not "are difficult" — cannot. Every breakwater, sea-gate and quay on the continent is Parsharan work, and nobody else can maintain it. That is why there are sanctions instead of an invasion: you cannot storm a country whose walls heal, and you cannot afford to lose the only people who can pour a breakwater.

Give the recipe a lock. Real pozzolana came from one district. Proposed: the "sea metals" are a specific sand from one Parsharan shore, and "the energy of sea" is not metaphor — it needs a spirit-blessed worker. The recipe is thus simultaneously a mineral monopoly and a religious one, and cannot be copied without both.

For the old god: Zoroastrianism grades sacred fire by how many fires made it.

  • Ātash Dādgāh — household fire; may go out.
  • Ātash Ādarān — embers from the hearths of four estates (priest, warrior, farmer, artisan); kept burning continuously.
  • Ātash Bahrām — embers from 1,001 fires, one from each trade in society, including fire struck by lightning; up to a year to consecrate.

PROPOSED — now that you've confirmed the old god is Aezion: Parshara keeps a fire that has never gone out, assembled from embers carried in from every trade in the kingdom. Not a statue, not an idol. The last surviving intact address of the creator.

One object gives you:

  • A religion that is also a constitution — every profession is inside the flame, so the fire is the nation.
  • A war aim that makes the blockade monstrous: under sanctions, what Parshara cannot afford is fuel.
  • A reason the Royal Scholar's post matters — Hayato is "responsible for preserving the very identity of Parshara," which now means keeping the fire's liturgy.
  • The reason nobody remembers Aezion: the fire is tended by a handful of people who may no longer know whose fire it is. They have fed a god for six hundred years without a name for him.
  • A physical thing Arin can be shown once and never forget.

And the killer detail: if Aezion shattered into four spirits, and Parshara's fire is the last intact piece, then Azevalgar's most valuable target is not a jewel. It is a flame that must be fed every day.

Priestly titles: Zoroastrian priests are athravan, later misread as "fire-priest" (etymology disputed). Available if you want something archaic that isn't a direct borrowing.

3.3 Aurorion

ESTABLISHED (vault, unusually complete). Once called Sama. No kingdoms in the Era of War. At the heart of the land — where the royal castle now stands after 600 years — stood an empty shrine, where lived a blind man whose remaining senses were so sharp he lived normally. He developed a method on the conviction that "a human was never meant to just crawl on the land and there must have been something which he can accomplish."

His words: "By concentrating on your breath, take in the air and feel it going through your nerves, next guide the coolness spreading in your chest towards your legs, contracting them and releasing moment after all the while holding your nerves in place, speeding up circulation in bursts. This shall ultimately give you mastery over 'Speed'."

The only known text from that era. With it: a diary. The diary's final entry:

"They are coming, many of them, they told me to close the shrine. But, How do I put it? They don't seem.....alive. Their voices are like a hiss of a snake and their touch, it is cold."

In the text: Aurorion bears a double-headed eagle. Its castle walls are built in marine concrete (ch 35) — which, per §3.2, means Aurorion's walls are Parsharan work. A kingdom at war with the sanctioned kingdom, defending itself with the sanctioned kingdom's technology. Either a contradiction or a very good secret.

Analogue: pranayama, and scripture-as-title-deed

The method is real physiology with a wrong conclusion. "Holding your nerves in place, speeding up circulation in bursts" is breath-retention with vasoconstrictive control — the territory of hatha yoga manuals, Tibetan tummo, and qigong. Plausible but wrong.

Use that. Aurorion's martial tradition rests on a successful empiricist who misdiagnosed his own discovery. Every technique descended from him inherits the error, and the error is why the tradition has a ceiling.

Second analogue — scripture as title deed. The Avesta, the Vedas, the I Ching: in each case one body of text from a lost age functions as the charter of a living institution.

PROPOSED: the royal family rules because it holds the only copy of the blind man's text. Not strength, not election — custody. A monarchy whose legitimacy is archival, which is thematically exact for a novel about a world under review, and it creates a vulnerability that costs nothing: burn one book and the dynasty is over.

The diary entry is the most important object in your setting. Six hundred years ago, at the founding moment of the greatest power on the continent, something arrived that was not alive, and a man whose senses were superhuman could not classify it. Not a man being frightened — a failure of category.

Now that Azevalgar is confirmed as an outer god, this has a destination: they were his, or the pale men's, or the Fog's — and the text has already shown you all three. Whatever they were, the enemy came to the shrine first, before the kingdoms, and the shrine was empty even then.

3.4 Vaeldor Principalities

ESTABLISHED. Shield emblem, north, inland behind the range, forested. Arin's diplomatic destination. "Still no design" — buildings "made in such a way that maximized usability and made no effort to look good." Frostgate is a frontier post where young men serve five years from sixteen (ch 41). Hearthglen's air is "absolutely opposite of Vaeldor principality" — warm and dry.

PROPOSED analogue: the fractious German principalities, or better, Irish/Scottish clan chiefdoms — many small lordships, no single throne, an aesthetic of utility because nothing survives long enough to decorate, and a frontier institution that doubles as a rite of passage and a dumping ground for surplus sons. The five-year service forges a shared identity across principalities that share nothing else.

Free detail: a garrison that turns over completely every five years cannot accumulate institutional memory. Anyone who has served longer than five years is either a criminal or a liar. One scene, free.

3.5 Dahan Sultanate

ESTABLISHED. Crescent emblem, Dahan City, arid south-central/west, borders Parshara, Aurorion and Remtor. Nothing else in the text.

PROPOSED analogue: the trans-Saharan caravan economy — camel trains of up to a thousand animals, oasis-to-oasis relay, salt-for-gold, merchants richer than the land they cross.

The strongest available idea: Dahan's wealth is water rights, not gold. Whoever holds the oases holds the crossings, and the oases are shrines. That merges caravan economy with religious institution and makes Dahan the natural rival to Parshara's fire — a nation of wells against a nation of flame, both holding something sacred that everyone else needs.

Part 4 — The real plot engine, already written

ESTABLISHED, ch 48. Vazir's diary, an entry two months prior:

"I don't know where these men have come from, but they gave me a choice. A choice where we could survive or even win back against the martial might of Aurorian Empire. It is just that they had two conditions which bugged me. First they wanted me to interfere with the trade routes of Parshara, a kingdom forcing the country to go bankrupt. Second was to let them deal with all affairs regarding Aurorian, Parshara, Velmor Duchies, Dahan Sultanate and Nerith kingdom overriding the authority of crown itself. …I don't understand their origin and why are they so fixated on 'Parshara kingdom' but they have agreed on leaving once their desired outcome is achieved."

The whole arc's machinery, already on the page. Read with the cosmology in hand:

  • The "men" are outside. No origin. They offered military salvation.
  • They demanded (a) bankrupt Parshara and (b) control of five sovereignties.
  • They are fixated on Parshara — where the fire is, and the only remaining intact piece of Aezion.
  • They promised to leave when the outcome is achieved. Not empire-building. Collecting something, then going home.

PROPOSED: these men are Azevalgar's. The sanctions are not Nazarana's policy — they are the enemy god's procurement programme, executed by a desperate king who thinks he is buying a war.

And Vazir knows something is wrong — "their origin," "so fixated" — but cannot see the shape of it, because he has been told the enemy is Aurorion. He is fighting the wrong war, on the wrong continent, for the wrong god, and the diary is the only place he has written it down.

Which means Vazir is not a villain. He is the man who signed the contract, and the arc's central tragedy is that he did it to save his people. It also makes him a perfect match for the younger princess of Aurorion: both are being destroyed by an agreement neither understood.

Part 5 — The lovers, and what the objective actually asks

ESTABLISHED (author). Vazir and the younger princess of the Aurorion Empire. Secret relationship. Both died before they could be together.

ESTABLISHED (ch 51).

[Re-Enacting: The Romance of the Four Kingdoms]
OBJECTIVE: Two lovers who come from different and unresolvable
           backgrounds couldn't unify in the past. Unify them.
EXTRA OBJECTIVE: ????
REWARD: ?????
PENALTY: Erasure.

Eldoris is not an exam — it is a re-enactment. Your overview note says the scenario tests "what kind of author a candidate is." The text says it replays a historical failure and asks whether it can be corrected. Correct that note.

The word that matters is "unify," not "reunite." They were never together. There is nothing to restore.

5.1 The four kingdoms of the title

The scenario is The Romance of the Four Kingdoms, and the lovers come from two of them. That is the title's own problem, and a gift:

PROPOSED: the four are Parshara, Aurorion, Nazarana and Dahan — the four powers on Vazir's own list, in the region where the map puts them. The others (Velmor, Galdwn, Nerith, Lorven, Eldrith) are the field on which the four collide.

The romance failed because it required four kingdoms to consent and only two people wanted it. That is why the failure was historical rather than personal — and why it cannot be fixed by putting two people in a room.

5.2 The problem the objective has, which the arc must solve

Both lovers are already dead. So "unify them" has at least three readings, and the choice determines the ending:

ReadingWhat Arin must doCost
A. Replayed alive — the present Vazir and present princess are the roles, and this time can succeedBring them together in the re-enactmentVazir is the man destroying Parshara. Uniting him with Aurorion's princess is treason on both sides.
B. Posthumous — they are dead and stay dead; Arin unites what they leftA marriage in death: joined houses, a shared shrine, a name spoken for bothHe completes the objective with a symbol, not a wedding. Cold, and very much this novel's register.
C. The re-enactment is the second chance — the fog replays the moment before their deaths, and Arin is inside itPrevent their deaths in the replay, knowing the original outcomeHe is not fixing a relationship. He is trying to un-die two people already dead, with the countdown running.

My read: C, with B as the twist. C explains why there is a countdown at all — a re-enactment has a runtime because it is a performance, and when the clock ends the performance ends and the original outcome stands. B is the answer to what "success" can mean when the people are dead, and it is the only version that lets the ending be true rather than sentimental.

But this is your call, and it is the largest single decision left in the arc. I will not write into any of the three without your word.

5.3 What you said about the run

Your instruction: "Taking them closer will only do good for Arin but he have to run now."

So Arin gets partial credit and must flee — he moves the lovers closer, earns something, and cannot finish. That is a good shape, and it has a consequence worth naming: the objective does not fail, it is abandoned mid-progress. If he can never return, the penalty is Erasure and the re-enactment resets. If he can return, the reader knows the objective stays open for a hundred chapters — a promise you have to be willing to keep.

PROPOSED: give him a visible, literal marker of partial progress — something that measurably moved. A letter delivered. A name spoken aloud. One of them weeping. A statue's head turned. Then when he runs, the reader has watched a thing almost happen, which is the most painful possible outcome and the one that sells the next arc.

Part 6 — Additions worth making

Ordered by value.

6.1 Shrines, jewels, and markers

A continent whose religion is fragment-worship should show fragments. Four jewels, four cities, one shrine in Tilmis where a shopkeeper kneels mid-transaction. The map should carry one marker; the plot should carry four.

6.2 The Book of Deeds

A physical ledger in each capital listing what the kingdom is owed and owes — with Kalman's Oath as the first entry, still the first line six hundred years later. It makes the state's moral condition a number, gives you bureaucratic magic consistent with a cosmology of Editors and review, and gives Arin a scene where he reads Nazarana's ledger and finds Parshara's name on every page.

6.3 Currency as occupation

Nazarana's coin is copper and universal (a boy sells food for thirty of them, ch 46); Parshara's people eat every other day. PROPOSED: the coinage is Nazaranan everywhere and Parshara's mint has been idle a generation, because the metal comes from Ganj. Every transaction in the starving kingdom uses the enemy's money. Currency as occupation, felt in the hand.

6.4 Four aesthetics, four theories of permanence

Vaeldor builds for use because nothing lasts. Nazarana builds to be seen because the market rewards it. Aurorion builds one thing and fills it with a book. Parshara builds to last forever, and so builds in water. One philosophical disagreement, showing up in the masonry.

6.5 Articles of manufacture Arin can pick up

The prose is generous with light and sensation; what is missing is objects. Each is a paragraph and each does a civilization's work:

  • A pozzolanic brick, still healing at the crack after a century in the water.
  • A Ganj touchstone — black, smooth, the only way to tell gold from gilded copper. The guild's real monopoly is not mining but certification: anyone can dig, only Ganj can say what a thing is worth.
  • A Tilmis postmark — an instrument, struck not written, that makes a letter real.
  • A Baazar seal — the physical form of the endowment: a mark meaning this cannot be sold.
  • A glowing stone, and the fact that it is warm.
  • A Frostgate service token, issued at sixteen, returned at twenty-one, worth nothing anywhere else.

6.6 Move the Order of Zenos out of Eldoris

Your vault files it as an Eldoris faction and the note admits the text does not support it. It should not be an Eldoris institution. A conspiracy inside the test can be solved by the test; a conspiracy that infiltrated from outside means the examiners are compromised — and you already have an outer god as antagonist. The Order belongs at world level, beside Azevalgar. Moving it costs nothing.

Part 7 — Decisions I need from you

Blocking, in order of weight.

  1. Which reading of "unify them"? A, B, or C. Recommendation: C with B as the twist, because C is the only one that justifies a countdown.
  2. Are the four jewels physically in the four Nazarana cities? If yes, Kalman's Oath is a contract with held collateral, the private armies make sense, and the heist structure exists.
  3. Do the four great spirits correspond to the four element glows (red/blue/green/brown, plus the rare white-grey), making Panak's brown earth and Tarkhan's water two of the four? Or are the great spirits a rank above the element spirits that Noor-e-Zaya belongs to?
  4. Are "the men" of ch 48 Azevalgar's? Everything in that diary reads as procurement.
  5. Does Aurorion's concrete come from Parshara? Ch 35 describes Aurorion's castle walls with visible pores, and your note says the material originates in Parshara. Either it is imported — a secret dependency in a war — or it is a continuity slip.
  6. Silvaris Donna (governor of Ganj) vs the Silvaris/Svitavar Enclave region. Which gets renamed?
  7. Do Lorven Republic, Eldrith Kingdom and the Svitavar Enclave appear in the planned arc? They are on the map with emblems and absent from the prose.

Part 8 — Method note

The map had to be read in crops: full-image vision analysis timed out five times, so it was split into a 520px overview plus north, north-west, north-east, south-west, south-east and Nazarana crops, converted to JPEG at 85–90 quality. That pipeline works and is re-runnable.

Region names were read inconsistently across passes (Svitavar/Sylvaris, GALDWN/Galdon, Solstheim/Solinar, and one-off reads of Toreth, Aethel, Remtor). Those should be confirmed by eye.
