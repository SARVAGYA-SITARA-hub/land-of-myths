A Room too Small for this World

1,805 words
Reading on

The first thing I saw when I opened my eyes was the ceiling.

Yellowed plaster, swollen where water had soaked through over the years, cracked like dried mud. A slow tick came from somewhere near the warped fan, like the whole room was being held together by habit instead of nails.

I breathed in. The air scraped through my chest like it had to fight for the space.

Cough.

It tore out of me on its own—a dry, ugly spasm that shook my whole body. Pain flared under my ribs. The room blurred, then settled.

It was a small room. One bed, more frame than mattress. A wooden stool doing the job of a bedside table. Shelves nailed together from mismatched planks, sagging under plastic containers and a few folded clothes. One grimy window let in grey afternoon light, and with it, the noise of traffic and the buzz of something electric outside.

"Arin!"

A woman leaned over me from the left. Dark hair tied back in a fraying band, deep lines carved around her eyes. She looked older than she probably was. Her hands were rough, the nails broken, stained at the tips with something like dye.

She pressed the back of her fingers to my forehead.

"Fever again," she muttered. "You just won't listen, will you?"

I knew that face now. That voice. In this life, she was my mother.

"Ma," I croaked. The word felt familiar and wrong at the same time,

"Don't talk." She reached for a dented kettle on the stove across the room. "Here. Drink. Slowly."

I pushed myself up on shaking elbows. My arms felt hollow, like someone had scooped out the muscle and left only skin and stubbornness behind. The thin blanket slid down. Ribs showed through my chest, each breath lifting them too sharply.

The cup touched my lips. Bitter, over‑boiled herbs, and under that, the chalky taste of cheap medicine. I drank, because fighting her would cost energy I didn't have.

While I drank, my mind kept working anyway. It always did.

This body is trash.

Not a complaint. A fact.

I went through it the same flat way I used to sort topics for an exam: weak lungs, probably scarred. A heart that thudded unevenly behind my sternum. Low muscle, no fat to spare. No marks on the skin—nothing to say I'd ever been touched by anything stronger than bad luck. Thirteen years old, and already running on empty.

In short, nothing about this body looked like it was built to survive whatever this world expected of it.

I let my eyes drift over the room. Concrete walls, paint flaking at the corners. The floor swept clean, though the cracks between the tiles still held old dust. On the far wall, an old screen flickered weakly, sound turned low, words crawling along the bottom.

"…disturbance near the Western Rim has settled… joint response alongside a visiting delegation… officials say there is no danger to nearby districts…"

The pictures didn't make sense to me. A stretch of ocean lit blue from underneath. People hovering above the water, arms out, light bending around their hands like it was listening to them. One name flashed under a man holding the air itself still in a shape no hand should be able to hold.

I had no memory that explained any of this. No story I could reach for. It wasn't déjà vu. It was just strange, the way anything is strange the first time you see it and have no shelf to put it on.

Then the rest of it came back, in pieces, the way water finds the lowest point in a floor.

A horn. Rain on a windshield. Headlights, too close, too fast.

I pushed that away. One thing at a time.

So. Not a dream. Not something I would wake from properly and laugh about later. A different body. A different mother. A different sky, apparently, with things living in it that bent light and stood on water. And me, in the middle of it, with lungs that couldn't survive a flight of stairs.

In my old life, I hadn't had anything special either. No money behind me, no family name that opened doors. Just a stubborn need to stop being invisible, and an exam I'd been chasing for years—the kind that tests everything a government could ask of a person, on the theory that if you can master all of it, you can be trusted with any of it. I'd buried myself in it. History, economics, the slow grinding logic of how a country governs itself. I hadn't passed. Death had gotten there first.

Now, somehow, I'd landed in a body too weak to climb stairs, in a world I didn't recognize at all.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then a thin line of light traced itself at the edge of my vision, coalescing into a translucent rectangle.

[STELLAR ARCHIVE — BOOT SEQUENCE…]

[Body compatibility: 23%]

[Recommendation: Stop making demands on this chassis.]

"What is this?," I muttered.

"Hmm?" Ma glanced back.

"Nothing, Ma," I said quickly. "Just… talking to myself."

Her expression tightened, but she turned away.

Is this a system?

The interface flickered as if responding. Words rearranged themselves with the flat politeness of official instructions:

[Yes, I am but a bigger and better version of what you are thinking about]

[Primary Directive: Guide user toward survivable threshold.]

[Secondary Directive: Grade user's life choices, Harshly.]

I'd seen systems in games that showered you with starter packs and glowing arrows. This one felt more like a bored examiner flipping through my answer sheet.

"Can you show me something?" I asked it silently. "Anything?"

There was a pause.

[Access denied.]

[Core functions locked until user demonstrates minimal narrative stability.]

"What does that even mean?" I breathed.

The Archive did not elaborate. Another line appeared instead:

[Unlock condition — Stage 1: Enter Institute of Inter world studies, General Track.]

I went still.

The Institute.

Here, the Institute exam seemed to be the only door not locked from both sides. And my system would not even fully boot until I walked through it.

"You're joking," I whispered to the hovering text.

No reply.

Of course.

On the screen, the segment shifted. Clean uniforms, training grounds, an anchor reading out dates in a bright, rehearsed voice. The Institute's name kept flashing under the footage.

"Ma," I said.

She glanced back. "Yes?"

"What is this Institute that keeps flashing in the news?" I asked, just to be sure.

She turned and looked at me, shocked, as if I had asked something obvious.

"The Institute of Interworld Studies," she began. "It is a school for higher education, which was established ninety years ago to research and nurture the changes that occurred in the world with that fog."

Fog. Changes. People standing on water.

She gave me a brief summary.

"Fog? Changes?" I asked.

"Did you forget? Your history teacher in middle school must have told you about it," she finished.

"Do we have any… papers? Books? About the Institute exam?" I changed the topic.

Her face shut like a door.

"Arin." Her voice dropped, careful and tired at once. "You know we're not—those coaching centres charge more in a month than I earn in two. We are not that kind of family."

"I'm not asking for coaching," I said, as gently as I could manage. "Just the syllabus. Old papers. Anything."

She didn't answer right away. Her hands had gone still over the stove.

"You used to ask me that," she said finally, quietly, like the words cost her something to let out. "Before. When you could still get out of bed on your own. You'd come home from the market with torn‑up pages someone had thrown out—half a page of some old guidebook, nothing useful—and you'd read them under the lamp until I made you sleep. You begged me for money for a proper book, once. I told you we didn't have it."

She stopped.

"I still don't have it."

I hadn't asked. He had.

Whatever was left of the boy whose body this was, whoever he'd been before I woke up wearing his ribs and his cough—he had already wanted this. Already understood, in a way I was only now catching up to, that this exam was the only door in this house that wasn't locked from both sides.

That changed something in me. Not because I needed a reason. Because now I had two of them, stacked on top of each other.

"There's the library," she said, before I could speak again. "City Central. Near the old university. They have machines you can use for free, but getting in costs something, and you—"

She didn't finish "you can't even walk to the corner without coughing yourself sick." She didn't have to. I heard it anyway.

"The library," I repeated.

She nodded, reluctant. "Big building. I've passed it on the bus. Never gone in. People like us don't really—" She stopped herself again, straightened her back like she was annoyed at her own sentence. "Forget it. Sleep. We'll talk later."

I let her think I'd dropped it. Inside my head, old habits were already lining themselves up like soldiers.

If there's a library, there are books.

If there's free access, there's a syllabus.

If there's a syllabus, there's a path.

There is always, somehow, a path.

I watched her move around the room—the way her shoulders dropped when she thought I wasn't looking, the extra second her hand rested on the doorframe before she made herself walk out to face another shift. I owed her more than dying quietly in this bed while she pretended she wasn't scared. I owed some version of myself—both of us, maybe, whoever we'd each been before this—more than letting one exam decide what I was worth without putting up a fight.

The Archive's window pulsed faintly at the edge of my vision.

[Warning: Current body structure fails multiple basic stability checks.]

[Attempting library run in this condition is statistically unwise.]

"Yeah?" I whispered. "So was deciding to prepare for UPSC with no backup plan. Look how that turned out."

I swung my legs over the side of the bed. My feet hit the cold floor. The room tilted. Black crept in at the edges of my sight. I waited it out, counting, breathing as steady as these lungs would allow.

Then I stood. Every joint complained. My chest burned. Sweat broke out cold along my spine. I took one step, one hand braced against the wall. Then another.

I had no money, no backing, no power. The only thing however I has was the mulish discipline that had once made me read the same Laxmikanth three times over, just to be sure.
