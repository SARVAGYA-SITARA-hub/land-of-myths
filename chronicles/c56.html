The Quiet Yard

1,275 words
Reading on

Knock. Knock.

I did not move. Whoever it was had knocked three times now.

The room breathed around me. Five soldiers, asleep, one of them snoring in a way that made the cot creak.

'If it were the governor's men, they would not knock,' I told myself. 'They would open it and hold a lamp to my face and ask me to take the helmet off, and that would be that.'

Knock. Knock.

I got up.

I did not put the helmet back on. In the dark, in a barracks, with the visor down, I was a shape and nothing else; with it off I was a boy with a swollen face, which is easier to look past than a stranger who will not show his own. I pulled the blanket over my shoulders like a man woken from a good sleep and annoyed about it, and opened the door.

A child stood in the corridor.

He could not have been more than twelve. Too-thin wrists, a wool coat three sizes too large with the sleeves rolled twice, and a smell coming off him that I knew well enough — horse, hay, and the particular sourness of a boy who has been up too long.

"...Sorry," he said. And then nothing. He had clearly prepared a sentence and lost it.

"Well?" I said. Unkindly. I meant to be unkind.

"The horses, sir." He looked at my boots rather than my face, which was a mercy. "The night man's gone to the wall. There's twenty-two of them and the feed's not down, and if it's not down by the bell the stablemaster will—" He stopped himself. "He'll not let me go home, sir. That's all."

"And the others?"

"Nobody's answering, sir."

I looked past him down the corridor. Windows on the left, grey stone, the long row of doors with their numbers painted badly by someone in a hurry. Every one of them shut.

Twenty-two horses. A garrison of two hundred men. A bell that had not rung.

'No one is answering,' I thought. 'In a building with two hundred men in it.'

Somewhere under my ribs, the thing I had been carrying since Parshara sat up and paid attention.

"The stablemaster sent you here?" I asked.

"He said anyone. He said get anyone."

"At this hour?"

"He said it hours ago, sir." The boy's voice cracked and he pretended it hadn't. "I've been knocking since."

I should have said no.

I want that written down plainly, because everything after this is my own fault and I would like the record to show that I knew. There is a version of that night where I closed the door, lay back down and was gone over the eastern wall before the bell. It was not a difficult version. It required nothing of me at all.

But the boy was standing in a corridor in wool three sizes too large, in the cold, being ignored by two hundred sleeping men, and I had been that boy. Not in this life. In the one before. There is a particular kind of tired that comes from being small and having to ask, and it does not leave you when you grow.

"...Lead," I said.

He looked up. "Sir?"

"Lead, before I change my mind."

*

The stable yard was on the northern side of the wall, tucked into the elbow where the garrison met the curtain, and the wind came down that elbow like water down a chute.

Twenty-two horses. I counted them twice, because counting is what I do when I am nervous, and got twenty-two both times.

The boy — his name was Ivan, and he apologized for it, which told me everything about how his month had gone — dragged a sack of feed off the cart and started on the near stalls. I took the far ones.

For a while it was fine.

The work was good. That surprised me. Grain, water, the specific weight of a horse's head coming down into your hand . A little hot breath in the cold. It was the first ordinary thing that had happened to me in some days, and I found I was doing it carefully.

Then I noticed the quiet.

Not the ordinary quiet of a walled yard at night. Not the held-breath quiet of a place where everyone is asleep.

Quiet.

I stopped with my hand on a bucket and listened — not hunting for a particular sound, but letting the whole of it arrive and sorting it. Wind in the slates. The curtain creaking. A horse shifting two stalls down. Ivan's boots, small and quick.

And nothing else.

No crickets.

The yard backed onto the western ditch, and the ditch ran the whole length of the outer wall, and every night of my life that I could remember — this life and the other one — a ditch in autumn had sound coming out of it. Water in a place, grass above it, and something living in between.

It was silent.

'Oh,' I thought, with a calm I did not feel. 'Someone has taken the crickets away.'

"Ivan," I said, and kept my voice ordinary. "How long since the last hourly bell?"

He thought about it. "Not since I started, sir. That's the thing. I thought I'd missed it and I—"

"Go inside."

"But the feed—"

"Go out, to the other stable wing. Now. Walk, don't run, and if anyone stops you say that you were sent to check the register to confirm the entries. Say it back to me."

"I was sent to check the register to confirm the entries for the day."

"Go."

He went. He looked back twice. I did not watch him the second time, because by then I had already turned around.

There was no one in the yard.

I want to be precise, because later I had to be. Eleven stalls down one side, eleven down the other, a cart, a feed store with its door hanging open, a gate to the wall walk. Two lanterns. A well-cover. Me.

No stablemaster. No guard.

I had been feeding horses for the better part of an hour with a man beside me, and I had not once asked myself who had unlocked the far stalls.

'Not now,' I prayed, and reached for it out of sheer habit. The world half-dissolved in my eyes, my senses came alive all at once, and there was nothing there. No thin pale line at the edge of vision. No grading. No warning. The Archive was not in this world. So the part of me that had been listening for it all evening, the part that had grown used to being told things, went suddenly and completely alone. And in the place where the warning should have been, I found that I already knew. 'Behind me.'

I threw myself forward.

Not a step. Not a dive. I put everything I had into being somewhere other than where I was, standing open in the air behind me, and I went through it face-first into the cobbles.

Behind me, the wall burst apart.

Not a hole. Not a crack. The stretch of wood and stones came back as splinters and one enormous block that turned twice in the air and landed four feet from my head hard enough to jump the ground.

Wind poured through. The horses screamed.

I lay on my face in the dirt and my senses which had heightened to its limit caught the sound of something slicing the air reaching for my neck behind me.
