Dear Mr. Valen,

On the recommendation of Chairman Mikhail Orlov Volkonskaya

(Zarya Consortium) , we are pleased to offer you admission to the

Institut d'Études Intermondiales (Institute of Inter-World Studies), Lyon, France, under the Inter-Civilizational Ambassadorial Fellowship Programme.

You have been selected for this cohort on the basis of:

– Your performance in the Academy General Track written examination and Ambassadorial Aptitude segment;

– Your demonstrated resilience and ethical reasoning under pressure;

– Your explicit preference for Ambassadorial studies in a year when many candidates sought Combat tracks and failed to qualify.

Due to an under-utilization of allocated Ambassadorial positions worldwide and a shortfall in Combat Study admissions, consortium partners have agreed to reassign certain seats to diplomatic training.

You are one of the few candidates to have genuinely prioritized Ambassadorial training as a first choice rather than as a fallback. On that basis, and following Chairman Orlov's personal recommendation, your candidacy has been forwarded for international placement.

If you choose to accept, your travel to France and tuition at the Institute will be fully sponsored under the joint Academy–Zarya Ambassadorial Initiative. Boarding and medical care will be provided in Lyon.

The words started to blur. I forced my eyes to focus.

You are requested to report to the departure terminal at Delhi International Gateport on 31st December 2031 for transport to Lyon on the same day. Detailed itinerary attached.

Please confirm your acceptance within 24 hours via the enclosed contact codes.

We look forward to your contribution to the future of inter-world diplomacy.

Sincerely,

Director, Institut d'Études Intermondiales (Lyon)

cc: Academy Ambassadorial Council, Zarya Consortium Liaison Office, Moscow.

My hand trembled.

Two days.

France.

Fully sponsored.

International placement.

All the words I had seen so many times in brochures and scholarship announcements in my first life, always attached to other people's names.

Ma whispered. "What does it say?"

I realized I'd gone silent halfway through.

I swallowed.

"They… want me," I said slowly. "For an institute in France. For Ambassador Studies. On a fellowship."

Ma stared.

"As in… out of country?" she said faintly.

"Yes."

"For… how long?"

"Years," I said. "Probably."

She sat back hard.

Megha let out a low whistle.

"Look at you," she said. "Local boy going foreign."

The security guard shook his head, a slow, impressed motion.

"The courier said something about 'global intake'," he said. "I thought he was just being dramatic."

Rao watched me over steepled fingers.

"Well," he said. "It appears the Academy has found you… inconvenient to categorize."

"Inconvenient," I repeated.

"They had too few Combat candidates good enough to risk on the front lines," he said. "Seats went unused. At the same time, the world is waking up to the fact that talking is cheaper than rebuilding cities. Orlov has been pushing for more investment in diplomats. This year, someone listened."

He nodded at the letter.

"You, inconveniently, are exactly what he argued for," he went on. "Someone who wanted this path, not as consolation, but as a first choice. Someone who doesn't flinch away when large men try to squash him like a bug."

"I flinched," I protested. "My knees flinched."

"Your eyes didn't," he said.

Ma found her voice.

"Two days," she said. "How can they expect… how can they just… take you like that?"

"It's an offer, Ma," I said. "Not an abduction."

She shot me a look.

"You think you have a choice?" she demanded. "After all this? You're going."

"Are you sure?" I asked softly.

She hesitated.

Something in her eyes shifted.

"I don't want… you to go," she said, each word dragged out. "But I want you there more than I want you here, dying slowly in front of that fan."

The fan in our room ticked in my mind at that exact moment, as if remembering its role in my origin story.

"You'll be… alone," she said.

"I've been alone in rooms full of people for years," I said. "This'll just be a different kind of alone."

She made a choked sound that might have been a laugh or a sob.

The Archive pinged me.

[This solves the 'medical facilities' problem,] it said. [France has decent hospitals.]

"Is that your way of saying congratulations?" I thought.

[No,] it said. [It is my way of saying you will not escape my commentary by crossing borders.]

Rao cleared his throat.

"We will need to prepare," he said. "Passports. Clearances. A suitcase. Possibly two shirts that aren't patched."

"Three," Megha said. "We can't send him to France with only one spare."

"I have no idea how to pack for another life," I said.

"Same way you packed for this one," Rao said. "Books. Stubbornness. Maybe a warmer jacket."

The room went very quiet for a moment.

Then the security guard, who had been watching all this with the expression of someone who couldn't decide if he was in a dream or in a bureaucratic mistake, stepped forward.

"Valen," he said gruffly. "When you first came to this library, you didn't even have twenty rupees."

"I remember," I said. "I still don't, most days."

He nodded at the letter.

"Next time you come back through that gate," he said, "I expect you to have more than twenty. In whatever currency they're using there."

"I'll try," I said.

"You better," he said. "I want to say I once turned away an international ambassador for lack of entry fee. Makes for a good story."

"I'll make sure you can tell it," I said.

The library around me was the same.

Shelves. Dust motes. The faint hum of terminal screens. The city outside was still Vasantpur, still smelling of dye and heat.

But my personal map had changed.

[You are leaving], the Archive said. [Again.]

"Looks like," I said.

[You failed to get into Mussoorie in your last life,] it said. [Now you are being exported to France. Not bad.]

"In fairness, my Willpower is an S," I assigned the grade to myself.

[Yes,] it said. [And it is about to be tested in new languages.]

I traced the edge of the letter with my finger.

"What do you think they want from me?" I asked it.

[The same thing every system wants,] it said. [Someone who will try to fix things instead of just noting how broken they are.]

"That sounds like your job," I said.

[I am an Archive,] it replied. [I record. You live. Don't mix up the roles.]

I folded the letter carefully and tucked it back into the envelope.

Two days.

France.

Institute of Inter-World Studies.

I had chased an exam across a bridge on failing lungs.

I had walked into a panel room and bled on the floor.

And somehow, in the rearrangement of vacancies and recommendations and guild politics, a cracked, underweight boy from Vasantpur had been considered… worth exporting.

I let out a breath I hadn't realized I'd been holding.

"Fine," I said, to the shelves, to Rao's office, to the city, to myself. "Let's see what the rest of the world looks like."

The Null Frame wrapped my lungs like a promise.

Stage Two: passed.

Stage Three: boarding.
