While He Slept

1,321 words
Reading on

[Archive POV]

The room was small.

One window, facing the building's inner courtyard.

One ceiling fan, slightly off-balance, ticking on every third rotation.

A stove in the corner. A chair. A bed, narrow, pushed against the wall — and on it, a boy who was not moving.

The Stellar Archive hovered above him.

Not visibly. Not in any way the woman who entered the room shortly after could perceive. It existed in the space between the system and the world, present the way a thought is present — without weight, without form, without anything that could be touched or seen or questioned.

It had been there since the steps.

It had watched the woman from the library call for a transit. Had watched the transit come. Had watched the boy loaded into it with the particular careful inefficiency of people who were not sure how fragile he was and were afraid to find out. Had watched him brought here, up three flights of stairs, by a neighbour whose name was not in any file the Archive held.

It had logged all of this.

It was still logging now.

The doctor arrived twenty minutes after the mother.

She had come home to find her son already in his bed — the neighbour again, a woman with a strong back and no patience for ceremony, who had explained in four sentences what had happened and left. The mother had stood in the doorway of the room for a long moment, looking at him.

Then she had called the doctor.

He was a small man. Methodical. He set his case on the floor by the bed and opened it with the ease of someone who had performed this motion ten thousand times, in rooms not unlike this one, with families arranged in postures not unlike hers — the particular stillness of someone waiting for the frame to settle before they could respond to what was inside it.

He ran the diagnostics reader across the boy's chest.

The Archive read the results before he did.

Left lobe: 31% functional capacity. Progressive scarring across the lower lobes, consistent with prolonged environmental exposure — industrial particulates, inadequate air filtration. Right lobe: compensating. Strain markers elevated. Compensation unsustainable at current rate.

The doctor studied his screen for a moment. Then he looked at the mother.

"The left lobe," he said quietly, "is operating at roughly thirty percent."

She didn't move.

"The right lobe has been compensating." A pause. "It won't continue to do so indefinitely."

The Archive noted: her hands. They had been loose at her sides. They were not loose anymore.

"How long," she said.

The doctor took a breath.

"With rest. Proper rest. No exertion. Adequate nutrition—"

"How long."

He stopped. Rearranged.

"A year," he said. "Possibly more."

The fan ticked.

Three rotations. Four.

"Possibly more," she repeated.

Her voice was flat. Not empty — the Archive had enough language data to distinguish between the two. Empty is the absence of something. Flat is what happens when something is present and being held very carefully so it doesn't spill.

"Yes," Doc-saab said. "At this age, the body still has—"

"How much more."

He stopped again.

The Archive processed his pause: 2.3 seconds. Longer than a standard conversational gap. Within the range associated with responses where the speaker is aware they do not have the information being requested.

"It depends on many factors," he said. "Living conditions. Rest. Whether the right lobe holds." He paused once more. "I have seen cases where—"

"Yes," she said.

One word. The word that means: I understand what you are not saying and I am choosing to take it from here.

The doctor closed his case.

"Mrs. Valen." His voice had dropped further, into the register the Archive classified as pastoral — the professional acknowledgement that the clinical portion of the visit was concluded and something else was now required. "He's a resilient boy. The fact that he was ambulatory at all, given the state of—"

"I know," she said.

A silence.

"Thank you, Doctor."

The door. The footsteps descending the stairwell, growing smaller.

Then: nothing.

Just the room. The fan. The boy. His mother.

She stood at the door for a moment after it closed.

The Archive observed: her shoulders. They had been held at a specific angle throughout the doctor's visit — back, level, braced. The kind of posture that is maintained, not natural. When the sound of the doctor's footsteps disappeared from the lower stairwell, her shoulders dropped approximately two centimetres.

Not a collapse. An exhale. The private version of something she had not allowed herself while he was present.

She crossed the room.

At the edge of the bed she stopped and looked at her son. The Archive did not have a category for the expression on her face. It ran a classification pass:

Grief: partial match. Exhaustion: partial match. Anger: negligible. Fear: elevated. Something else—

The classification returned: insufficient data.

She reached out and touched his forehead with the back of her fingers. Checked the temperature the way she'd done before he could remember — before this life, certainly, and perhaps before the habits needed a reason. Her hand stayed there. Longer than a temperature check required.

The boy's face did not change. He was very far under.

She pulled her dupatta tighter across her shoulders. Sat in the chair. Folded her hands in her lap.

And that was all.

No crying. No words. She simply sat with what she had been given and held it in both hands and stayed.

The Archive observed this for a long time.

It ran no classifications.

At some point — forty-seven minutes later, by the Archive's log — the mother stood. She moved to the kitchen. Water. The mechanical rhythm of hands doing small tasks.

The room was quiet now except for the boy's breathing.

The Archive hovered above him.

It had been designed as an evaluative system. Classification, tracking, ranking — these were its functions. It did not give. It measured and withheld and released according to protocols established at its creation. Karmic coins accrued through narrative impact: problems solved, expectations exceeded, change created in the world.

The boy had created no change today.

He had walked across a bridge on lungs that should not have carried him that far. He had asked a guard a question the guard had no good answer to. He had fallen on library steps having accomplished nothing, obtained nothing, moved nothing in the world from one state to another.

Technically.

The Archive reviewed its own protocols.

Then it reviewed the day.

The railing gripped like a lifeline. The twenty-rupee gap. I'm not asking for charity — do you have a labour exchange program? The diagnostics reader results it had seen before the doctor did and said nothing.

The Archive processed this for a long time.

Then, without ceremony, it generated a notification.

It hung in the air above the sleeping boy — visible to no one in the room, unreachable by the unconscious figure it was addressed to, undetectable by the woman in the kitchen.

[ 1,000 Karmic Coins Obtained ]

The Archive remained still.

Then, in the same silence, in the same empty room, it added one more line.

No brackets. No formatting. The kind of line that is not a system output — the kind that happens when something with rules reaches the edge of them and takes one step past, just once, just here, and knows it while it does it:

[this will be the first and last time.]

The notification stayed.

The boy breathed.

In the kitchen, water ran.

After a moment, the notification folded inward and disappeared. The Archive settled back into observation mode. The room returned to what it was: small, dim, a ceiling fan ticking slightly off-rhythm, a boy sleeping in a narrow bed against the wall.

Nothing had changed.

Somewhere in his account, a number had.

He would not know why for a long time.

Perhaps he never would.
