The Letter

1,544 words
Reading on

The first thing I saw when I woke up was the ceiling.

Different ceiling.

White, too clean, with soft panels instead of yellowed plaster. No fan. Just a vent humming somewhere above, pushing air that smelled of antiseptic and filtered recycled something.

I tried to sit up.

My ribs filed a complaint. The Null Frame flared reflexively, then eased when it realized we weren't in the interview room anymore.

[Body Status: Fragile but not immediately dying.] the Archive reported. [Congratulations on surviving yet another poor decision.]

"Water," I croaked.

A hand slipped behind my neck, lifting my head gently. A plastic cup touched my lips.

"Slowly," a voice said. Male, brisk, professional.

I drank. Coolness slid down my throat, bringing back scraps of memory with it.

Orlov's pressure. My knees. The taste of blood. The floor rushing up.

"Hospital?" I managed.

"Infirmary," the doctor corrected. "Parliament Annex, medical wing. Close enough."

He checked the displays hovering near the bed.

"Pressure-induced micro-ruptures in already compromised lung tissue," he said. "Not the worst I've seen. Not the best either. You'll live. Annoyingly."

"Panel?" I asked.

He snorted. "They're not allowed in here. Interview's over. Go home. Wait for the result like everyone else."

Of course.

I closed my eyes.

"Your librarian friend was here," the doctor added. "Refused to leave until I told him you'd stabilized. The older gentleman with the sharp tongue?"

"That sounds like him," I said.

He moved away, footsteps soft on the padded floor.

The Archive hovered closer.

[That was reckless,] it said. [Even by your standards.]

"Was it worth it?" I whispered.

[You stayed conscious longer than you should have,] it said. [You did not break eye-contact. You did not beg. For someone with your medical file, that counts as… notable.]

"Notable doesn't equal selected," I said.

[No,] it agreed. [But it equals remembered.]

I wasn't sure if that was comforting.

Rao arrived an hour later.

The infirmary door slammed open hard enough to make the nurse jump.

"Where is he?" he demanded.

"Sir," I said weakly, lifting a hand.

He moved towards and then sat on the chair near my bed

"The doctor said they'll discharge you by evening," he said. "And then we return at night."

"How long was I out?" I asked.

"Only a few hours," he said.

He sat on the edge of the bed, hand resting on my forearm.

"Did you… answer well?" he asked quietly.

"Not at first," I admitted. "I tried to be someone else. Then I failed at that too. Then I talked like myself. Then Orlov tried to crush me. Then I redecorated their floor."

He closed her eyes briefly.

"You and floors," he muttered. "Always meeting each other at important times."

I laughed, then coughed, then regretted both.

"I'm sorry," I said.

"For what?" he asked sharply.

"For worrying you." he was quiet for a moment.

He sighed

"At least this time you were falling in the right direction," he remarked.

We went back to Vasantpur that night.

The train rattled, lights dimmed. I dozed against the window, feeling the slight ache in my chest with every breath, the way Null Frame now wrapped not just around old scars but fresh bruises too.

Rao sat opposite, arms folded, eyes closed. He looked like he was sleeping. but he wasn't.

He opened his eyes.

"You understand," he said, "that whatever happens now is no longer in your hands."

"I never liked that," I said.

"Then focus on what is," he replied. "Let the Institute take its time proving whether or not it is worthy of you."

"That's not how the power dynamic works," I said.

"In my library," he said, "it does."

Days turned into weeks.

Life slid, with uncomfortable ease, back into its old pattern. Morning cleaning. Afternoon study. Evening revision. Night listening to Ma talk about factory gossip.

The only difference was the silence.

No new notifications from the Institute. No letters. No calls.

The Archive checked the portal once a day, unprompted.

[Interview Results: Pending.]

After two weeks, the notification changed to:

[Interview Results: Processed. Candidates will be contacted individually.]

"Individually" turned out to mean "everyone but you, apparently."

"Maybe the letter got lost," Ma said once, more out of loyalty than conviction.

"The Academy does not lose letters," Rao said. "They misplace entire lives, but paper? Never."

After four weeks, even the Archive stopped volunteering updates.

"Statistically," I said one afternoon, wiping down a table, "the absence of a result is a result."

[It could also be bureaucracy. Or incompetence. Or someone forgetting to click 'send.'] it said

"Comforting," I said.

"How are you?" Megha asked from the front desk.

"Disappointingly stable," I said.

She snorted.

"You'll get in," she said. "People who faint that spectacularly in front of important men never get forgotten. Even if they want to."

"Maybe they decided they don't want a boy who decorates their floors," I said.

"Then they're stupid," she replied, and went back to her cataloguing.

At the one-month mark, I stopped checking the portal.

Maybe I had failed. Maybe Orlov's pressure test had been more than my body could afford. Maybe the panel had decided that sending a boy with half a lung to talk to dangerous entities was a liability they couldn't justify.

I was tired.

Not of trying.

Of hoping.

[If you want to be sad about this, schedule it like anything else. Half an hour. Then move on.] Archive said one evening.

"That's not how feelings work," I said.

[It worked for you with mock test results,] it said. [You would sulk, eat something sweet, and then plan the next attempt.]

"I like how you remember the sweets," I said.

[I remember everything,] it replied. [Including the part where you swore you'd rather die than give up. You've done the first. Don't do the second.]

"I'm not giving up," I said. "I'm just… accepting that maybe this door doesn't open for me."

[And if another door opens?]

"I'll check if there's an exam at the end," I said.

The door that opened was Rao's office door.

Literally.

It was a slow afternoon. I was in Reading Hall A, alone except for a couple of college students pretending to study and actually scrolling their feeds. The tub of abandoned books was half-full. I was enjoying the rare silence.

"Arin!" Megha's voice cut across the hall. "Rao-ji is calling you. Office. Now."

"Did I mis-shelve something?" I called back.

"Probably," she said. "

Her tone was… odd.

I parked the tub, wiped my hands on my trouser legs, and headed to the office.

The glass wall was fogged. Someone had activated the privacy setting. That almost never happened.

I knocked.

"Come," Rao's voice said.

The door slid open.

The room was full.

Megha stood to one side, arms folded, trying and failing to look casual. The security guard from the library entrance—smart-glasses, worn patience, the first man I'd ever asked about labour exchange—leaned against a shelf, hands behind his back like he wasn't sure what he was doing here.

And my mother sat on the chair opposite Rao's desk, dupatta smoothed so much it looked like it might disintegrate. Her eyes were wide, not with fear this time, but with something wired and brittle.

"Ma?" I said. "What are you doing here? You have shift—"

"They let me come," she said. "Rao-sir sent a message. Said library emergency."

"I'm not on fire," I said.

"Not that kind of emergency," Megha muttered.

On the desk, between Rao and my mother, lay a large cream envelope with too many stamps and too much importance.

My heart did something unhelpful.

Rao gestured to the remaining chair.

"Sit, Valen," he said.

I sat.

The envelope stared at me.

"So," I said, because someone had to break the silence. "Either I've been banned from the library for reshelving sins, or—"

"Open the letter, boy," the security guard said, sounding uncharacteristically impatient.

I glanced at him.

"You know what it says?" I asked.

"I know who brought it," he said. "Courier in a suit so stiff even the scanners stood up straighter. Didn't even want to leave it at the front desk. Said 'hand-delivery only'."

"Hand delivered to whom?" I asked.

"To you," Rao said. "But since your presence seemed… unreliable, I took custody."

He slid the envelope toward me.

My fingers felt clumsy as I picked it up. The paper was thick, the kind that crinkled softly instead of tearing. The address was handwritten.

To

Mr. Arin Valen

c/o City Central Public Library

Vasantpur

A different symbol in the top left corner: a stylized map with radiating lines. Underneath, in small print: Institut d'Études Intermondiales, Lyon.

France.

My brain took a moment to process the idea that a place I had previously known only from maps and UPSC geography questions was now sending me mail.

"Rao Sir," I said slowly. "Is this some elaborate prank?"

"If it is, it is a very well-funded one," he said. "Open it."

I broke the seal.

Inside, there were two things: a formal letter on heavy paper, and a second, thinner sheet that looked suspiciously like a flight ticket.

I unfolded the letter.
