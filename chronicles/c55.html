Bird in the Cage (I)

1,981 words
Reading on

{Arin’s POV}

I lay on my back in the dirt with my eyes closed and my mouth slightly open, and tried very hard to look like a man who had been kicked off a griffin.

It wasn’t difficult. My armor was dented where I had thrown myself against a tree. There was mud on the breastplate and a leaf stuck on my shoulder guard. I had even smeared a little blood from the first soldier’s nose on the side of my helmet, which felt like going a bit too far, but ‘a careful liar leaves nothing to chance’.

The beating of wings grew louder. Wind pushed through the trees and the leaves around me rustled.

‘Dozens. Maybe two dozen.’

The thud of claws on the soil followed one after another. Armor clanked. Someone barked an order and boots rushed towards me.

“Here! One of ours is here!”

“Is he alive?”

A gauntleted hand grabbed my shoulder and shook me. I let my head roll to the side, counted to three in my head, and then coughed.

“Khuh…khugh…”

“He’s alive! Get some water!”

Hands pulled me up into a sitting position. Someone pressed a flask against the gap of my helmet, and I took a small sip while keeping my visor down. A man with a plume of grey on his helm crouched in front of me. Captain, going by the way the others stood stiff around him.

“Report, soldier,” he asked. “What happened here? Where are the other two from your unit?”

I had thought about this part for a long time while lying in the mud. Hayato-san was heading to Somalis, and there was a part of me that screamed at the idea of putting that word in this man’s ears. But the pursuers would sweep the south anyway, since it was the obvious road for two fugitives from Parshara.

‘Give them the road they already expect. And hope the one I walk off is the one they don’t look at.’

I lowered my voice and roughened it, dropping the western accent and trying the eastern one. “They…went down into the forest, sir. I saw them hit the other two. One of them kicked me off my mount before I could blow the whistle.” I coughed again, because it seemed right. “They rode south. Towards Somalis.”

“Both?”

“Yes, sir.”

The captain stood and turned to his men. “Third and fourth pack, to Somalis! Sweep the road and the villages around it! Send word to the governor of Somalis and tell her to close the gates.” Then his gaze turned back to me and the other fallen soldier two trees away who was groaning with his hand on his nose. “The rest, we escort the wounded to Baazar. It’s the closest garrison.”

I bowed my head and let out a breath no one could hear.

‘Baazar. West, as far from Somalis as I could hope for. And the last place two fugitives from Parshara would go.’

Which is exactly what makes it a bad place to be.

There was no choice, though.

“Can you stand?” a voice asked.

The hand which had been holding me up belonged to a young man with a round face and a crooked nose, who looked like he had been broken once and set badly. He pulled my arm over his shoulders before I could answer and half-dragged me towards one of the griffins.

“Come on, you’re riding with me. Dorvan,” he said, tapping his own chest. “You?”

‘Name. Name. The armor.’

I remembered the name done in a neat artistry on the inside of the collar.

“…Rhen,” I said.

“Rhen. Alright, Rhen, hold on tight.”

The griffin turned its head the moment I came near. One golden eye locked on me. A low sound rolled out of its throat, and wings flared.

‘Of course. Earlier too. They don’t like me.’

“Easy, girl, easy!” Dorvan patted its neck.

We rose.

The forest dropped below us in a green sea, and I clutched the leather strap with both hands, which wasn’t acting. I had jumped from towers and ridden on thin air, but I had never in my life been carried by something that actively disliked me.

“Two of them, and they dropped three of you?” Dorvan shouted over the wind. “Monsters. Absolute monsters.”

“Yes,” I said. “Monsters.”

He was silent for a few breaths, and then his voice changed. “Is it true? About the Queen?”

I frowned, My fingers tightening on the strap.

‘Queen? Did something happen?’ I thought. But the gears in my mind turned quickly.

I made a statement. Neither confirming nor denying.

“That’s what we were told, when we were woken u urgently to find the fugitives.”I said. After listening to me he continued.

“Her Majesty is dead and the envoys from Parshara did it. I couldn’t believe it. You know my family was in debt, Two years ago, my father’s workshop in Baazar, he made lamp fittings. The guild was going to take it. And then the Queen cut the lamp tax for small workshops. Just like that. My father cried in the kitchen when he heard.” He laughed, a short, embarrassed laugh. “I thought, what kind of queen cuts a tax like that? And now…”

He didn’t finish.

I stared at the back of his helmet, my eyes wide in disbelief. I could not say a single word.

‘She is dead?......’

‘She was shrewd. I knew that. She sat in front of Hayato-san and me with the lavender eyes and the red robe and did calculate profit and loss of the alliance in her head faster than we drank the water in front of us. And she was alive. And now…’

I swallowed it down.

Like that Dorvan kept talking all the way till we reached Baazar.

I didn’t reply.

*

Baazar rose out of the plains, breathing like a living giant.

We came in over the western walls in late afternoon. Smoke pipes stood out in the forests of buildings and houses above rooftops of slate and tin. Banners, hundreds of them hung like advertisements. Under us ran streets so crowded that I could see them moving like rivers of ants, and even from that height I could hear the clang of hammers and the cries of merchants.

‘Baazar. The west hub of Nazarana thrived off of merchants and tourists and buyers all around the continent.’

The garrison was built into the wall itself, with a tower for the griffins and long barracks of grey stone behind it. We landed in a yard of packed earth. Nurses took the other fallen soldier away on a stretcher. I waved them off, claiming that nothing was broken, and a harried quartermaster with ink-stained fingers thrust a wooden token into my hand.

“Wing C, room nine. Dinner is served in the room. Governor’s orders, everyone eats in their quarters tonight. Report at dawn.”

“Orders for what, sir?” I asked.

He looked at me like I was an idiot. “War, soldier. What else?”

Dorvan clapped my back, hard enough to rattle my armor. “Get some rest, Rhen. I’m in room six, if you need anything.” He grinned, and walked off whistling.

I stood there for a moment, holding a wooden token and wearing a stranger’s name.

‘Dorvan,’ I thought. ‘Don’t be so kind. It makes this difficult.’

Room nine was a stone box with a long table, eight cots, and one small window. Five soldiers were already there, and the smell of lamb stew and flat bread filled the air. They glanced up when I came in. I raised a hand in greeting and sat at the end of the table, where the lamp barely reached.

The helmet was the problem.

A bowl was put in front of me and I looked at the spoon and thought, with some desperation, ‘How do I eat with a helmet on?’

“Something wrong with your head, mate?” a bearded man across the table asked, around a mouthful of bread.

“Kicked in the face,” I mumbled. “It’s swollen. I don’t want…you know.” I gestured vaguely at my own visor. “Not pretty.”

There was a pause, and then the man snorted. “Ha! Fair enough. Keep it on, then. Nobody wants to look at that while eating.” The others laughed. I raised the visor a hand’s breadth, just enough for the spoon, and kept my face in the shadow of the rim.

The stew was too salty. I ate it anyway.

For a while they spoke of the price of wine, a woman at the Rusty shop near the garrison, a bet gone wrong. I listened and chewed and let the voices wash over me, and then, the way water finds a crack, the talk turned.

“Did you hear the governor smashed a whole table this morning?”

“A whole table? Gold?”

“Gold. Broke it into pieces with one fist. My cousin is a guard in the east wing, he saw the thing. Says the governor was shaking.”

“Can you blame him? The Queen…”

“Shh, quiet.”

“No one’s listening. I’m only saying, if I were that Panak, I’d be breaking more than a table.”

I kept my spoon moving.

“I heard the Supreme Commander came in on a griffin an hour after noon,” a thin soldier with a pockmarked face said, lowering his voice. “Straight from the capital. They say he didn’t even stop to wash.”

“Tarkhan? Here?”

“And that’s not all. I saw a woman at the gate last night, red coat, two curved swords. The innkeeper at the Anvil said it was the Crimson Gale herself. Sellen Orath.”

Someone whistled. “The Crimson Gale? She’s not cheap. Who paid her?”

“Who do you think?”

They went silent. A spoon scraped a bowl. Then the bearded man leaned in. “Heard the gates are sealed too. Outbound only. No one leaves Baazar without the governor’s seal.”

My spoon stopped.

“Sealed? The whole city?”

“Since noon. Merchants are screaming. Governor doesn’t care.”

“Well. Those Parshara bastards can’t have gotten this far.”

“No. But if they have…” The bearded man drew a finger across his throat. “Governor will have them before the next sunrise.”

They laughed, grimly, and the talk went back to wine.

I looked down at my bowl and found that I wasn’t hungry anymore.

‘The Supreme Commander. A famous mercenary. A sealed city. In one afternoon.’

The governor had not wasted a single hour.

*

I lay on a cot with my hands behind my head, staring at the dark ceiling. The others had blown out the lamp one by one and now the room was filled with deep, steady breathing. Someone snored. Moonlight fell in a thin silver line through the window.

‘Think.’

The city gates were sealed. That meant I couldn’t walk out, and a soldier of the aerial unit, whose griffins hated him, could not simply fly out either.

‘The Cloth,’ I thought. ‘It erases my presence. For a short time. At the gate, it might work.’

‘Null Craft. Twenty seconds of empty space at a time. The wall is high. And I may not find a cover like previous time. ’

I turned over the possibilities one by one like cards, and every one of them ended in the same place: a lot of people looking at a stranger who shouldn’t be there.

‘One step at a time,’ I told myself again. It had sounded wise in the forest, but at this hour there was nothing more foolish that being the rat in a cage thinking that it was safe.

Knock. Knock.

I froze.

I counted five breaths, and the knock came again, soft and polite.

Knock. Knock.
