Stage One Clear

1,622 words

Results did not arrive for the next few days

The Institute apparently seemed to follow the principle that suspense strengthens character.

Days stretched.

I went back to my cleaning schedule, my notes, my unofficial oral exams with Rao.

Ma pretended not to check the Academy portal every night.

The Archive, traitor that it was, helpfully tracked the days since exam.

[Days Since Written: 5.]

[Days Since Written: 9.]

[Days Since Written: 14.]

At the fifteen-day mark, as I was shelving a stack of foreign civilization texts, a new notification cut through the usual background hum of Archive messages.

[Institute Written Examination – Results Released.]

I froze.

**[Candidate: Arin Valen]**

[General Track – Status: 170/200- Passed]

[Ambassador Studies & Delegation Aptitude – Status:390/500- Passed .]

[Overall Standing: Within Interview Cut-off Range.]

[Next Step: Panel Interview Scheduling – Notification Pending.]

For a moment, the words blurred.

Passed.

Not topped. Not ranked. Not yet. Just one simple, heavy word.

Passed

[Host] the Archive said quietly. No sarcasm, no commentary.

"Yeah?" I whispered.

[Good Job]

"Valen?"

Rao's voice floated over from behind me.

I turned.

He stood there with a thin print-out in his hand. The Academy must have sent official lists to institutional centers; of course the library was on that distribution tree.

He looked at the paper, then at me.

"Arin Valen," he read. "General Track: pass, Ambassadorial aptitude: pass. Called for interview."

He lowered the sheet.

"Well," he smiled. "It seems my blood pressure preparations were not wasted."

Ma would be at the home right now, thinking about what to make for dinner.

As I turned to run home

"Valen"

I paused.

"You wrote 'when I pass' in that notebook," he said.

"I saw it, Terrible handwriting."

Heat rushed to my face.

"You weren't supposed to—"

"Next time," he said, "leave more space after the 'when.' Life has a habit of adding things you didn't plan for."

He waved a hand, as if dismissing me for a trivial errand.

"Go," he repeated.

I went.

Outside, the city hummed the same as it always had.

But under it, something else had shifted.

Telling my mother I had passed was harder than answering any ethics case study.

With the system notification still hovering faintly at the edge of my vision, I took the stairs to our room two at a time. That alone would have been a miracle six months ago. Now, the Null Frame rose and fell without complaint, a quiet harness around my lungs as if it had been waiting for this climb.

The door was unlatched. I pushed it open.

Ma sat on the floor by the stove, sorting pulses into a steel container. The fan ticked overhead in its usual off-beat rhythm.

"Ma," I said.

She didn't look up immediately. "You're home early," she said. "Rao-sir threw you out again?"

"No," I said. "He… dismissed me. Officially. For the day."

That got her attention.

She straightened, palms dusty with flour, eyes narrowing. "Why? Are you ill?"

"Not ill," I said quickly. "Just… read this."

I held out my report card.

She wiped her hands on her dupatta, took the card.

INSTITUTE WRITTEN EXAMINATION – RESULTS

CANDIDATE: ARIN VALEN

GENERAL TRACK – STATUS: PASSED

AMBASSADOR STUDIES & DELEGATION APTITUDE – STATUS: PASSED SHORTLISTED FOR INTERVIEW

Her lips moved as she read, the words forming silently before they reached comprehension.

Then she Froze.

The fan ticked. Outside, someone shouted the price for vegetables. Somewhere in the building, a child laughed.

Ma blinked once. Twice.

"Passed," she said, as if testing whether the word would hold.

"Yes," I said.

"For sure," she added, like there might be a second page that said "just kidding."

I pointed at the screen. "Unless they've started issuing emotional damage pranks through the civic network, yes. It's real."

She stared at the report card a moment longer.

Then she exhaled slowly, like air had been trapped in her chest for years and had only now been granted an exit permit.

"I told the supervisor you would," she said.

"You what?"

She sniffed, looking offended. "He said, 'Madam, results takes time; why you are worrying so much?' I told him, 'My son will pass. You keep giving me extra shifts, and when he gets into the Institute, I'll take all my sick leave at once.'"

"That is not how sick leave works," I said.

"Fine," she said. "I'll fall ill properly that time. Happy?"

The corner of my mouth twitched.

She wiped at her face with the edge of her dupatta, pretending it was sweat.

"Interview also," she said. "There is interview, no?"

"Yes."

"You will pass that also," she said, with the tone of someone filing a complaint directly to the universe.

Then, more quietly, "You have to. I don't have a plan for… not."

I sat down opposite her.

"Ma," I said. "We didn't have a plan for 'not' last time either. I still survived."

She glared, then let out an unwilling laugh that sounded like it had to fight past too many other emotions.

"You tell him?" she asked after a moment.

"Rao?" I nodded. "He had a printed list before I even saw the notification. I think the Academy sends him results faster than they send them to candidates."

"Of course," she murmured. "He's a librarian. Knowledge respects seniority."

"Is that how it works?" I asked.

"Don't argue with me when I'm proud of you," she said sharply.

She reached across, caught my chin in her hand, and turned my face side to side like she was checking if passing an exam had done anything to my bone structure.

"You still look the same," she said. "Just more… there."

"More… what?"

"More you," she said, waving a hand as if that explained everything.

Then she let go.

"Wash up," she said briskly. "We'll go to the library. You'll say thank you properly. Then you'll start preparing to talk to important people without sounding like a smart-mouthed idiot."

"That's… specific," I said.

"Yes," she said.

The first thing Rao did when he saw me back in the library was not congratulate me.

"Valen," he said, glancing up from a stack of returns. "I thought I dismissed you for the day."

Ma hovered behind me, suddenly shy in the cool, filtered air. She folded and unfolded the edge of her dupatta.

"Sir," she said. "He… passed."

"So the paper says," Rao replied.

He studied me for a long moment. His expression didn't change much, but something in his gaze did—like he'd taken me down from a shelf labeled "hypothetical" and moved me to "currently in circulation."

"Well done," he said at last. "You have successfully convinced a panel of overworked examiners that you can read and think under pressure."

Ma made a small outraged noise. "Sir, it is not just—"

"It is exactly that, Mrs. Valen," Rao said mildly. "And it is not small."

He shifted his attention back to me.

"Do you know what happens next?" he asked.

"Interview," I said. "Mixed representation from Institute and guilds."

"And?" he prompted.

"And they decide whether I'm worth giving a bed and three meals a day," I said. "Or whether I should continue my current diet of dust and humility."

"That is the practical translation, yes," he said. "But what does the interview actually measure?"

"Knowledge?" I ventured. "Awareness of current affairs. Understanding of politics. Ability to reason through scenarios."

"That is what the written measured," he said. "The interview measures whether you can do any of that while inhabiting a body and a social space at the same time."

I frowned. "Meaning?"

"Meaning," he said, "you will not be a disembodied answer sheet in that room. You will be a breathing, visibly fragile adolescent claiming a seat in a track usually filled with polished guild-trained candidates. They will watch what you say, but more importantly, how you say it. How you sit. Where your eyes go. When you choose to remain silent."

Ma bristled. "Fragile? He is much stronger now."

"I know," Rao said calmly. "But the panel's eyes will still see ribs before resolve, unless he teaches them otherwise."

He turned fully to me.

"Written exams are about what you know," he said. "Interviews are about what you are."

"That seems unfair," I said.

"Of course it is," he said. "That is why you must walk in having already decided who you are. Or they will decide it for you."

The Archive stirred in the back of my mind.

[Interview: Narrative Assessment Module.] [Risk: High. Opportunity: Higher.]

"Any practical advice?" I asked, trying not to show how my palms had just started sweating.

"Yes," Rao said. "When the schedule comes, bring it to me. We will see how much time the Institute has given you to become presentable."

He looked at Ma.

"Mrs. Valen," he said. "Your son has done well. But if you praise him too much now, he will float into the interview room and forget to sit down."

Ma's eyes narrowed. "As if he listens."

"Then perhaps he will listen to this," Rao said, addressing me again. "Passing written proves you can serve. Interview proves you can represent. Those are not the same skill."

"I'll… learn," I said.

"You have been learning since the day you walked into my library and fainted on the steps," he said dryly. "Go home. Wait for the scheduling notice.

The scheduling notice arrived three days later, without fanfare and with the efficiency of a bureaucratic guillotine.

I was at the library, midway through wiping down Reading Hall B tables out of habit more than obligation, when the Archive interrupted my internal rant about someone's abandoned tea cup ring.

[Institute Interview Scheduling Notification – Received.]
