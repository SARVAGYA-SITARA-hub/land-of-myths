Interview - II

1,684 words

Delhi did not smell the way I remembered.

In my first life, it had been dust, exhaust, street food, and sweat—compressed ambition under a humid sky.

In this one, the sky above the capital held the faint metallic texture of barrier fields, layered over the same old exhaust and ambition.

[Location: Delhi – Central Administrative Sector.]

[Warning: Probability of bureaucratic nonsense: extremely high.]

"Comforting," I muttered.

Rao adjusted the strap of the worn leather bag on his shoulder. He wore his usual shirt and trousers, ironed into reluctant obedience, and a slightly better pair of shoes than usual.

"Stop staring at the buildings like a tourist," he demanded.

Ahead of us, the Central Annex of Parliament rose from the stone like someone had taken all the seriousness in Delhi and built a monument with it. Sandstone and glass, old and new, stitched together by blue circuitry veins. Flags fluttered from the frontage—Institute crest, national emblem, and a guild banner I recognized from too many news segments.

It bore a stylized sun over a fractured horizon.

Zarya Consortium.

Russian-heritage guild. One of the top three in the world when it came to cross-civilizational logistics and "special operations," which was polite language for "we go places others die trying to reach."

"Zarya's here," I said, trying to keep my voice neutral.

"Mm," Rao said. "Their chairman is on the Ambassadorial oversight panel this year."

We joined the line of candidates snaking toward the Annex security gate.

Ma had hugged me so hard before I left Vasantpur that the Archive had logged a brief oxygen spike.

"Don't argue with them," she'd said. "Don't argue with yourself either. And if you faint, make sure you fall forward. At least then they'll remember your face."

"Such faith," I'd told her.

Now, that faith sat beside me in the form of one aging librarian who had absolutely no obligation to accompany a factory worker's son and had done it anyway.

"Valen," he said, "Once you cross that gate, I cannot help you. My job is to make sure you arrive at it. Upright."

He fixed me with his examiner look.

"You will go in," he said. "You will make an honest attempt to show them who you are. If they fail to understand, that is their exam to retake, not yours."

Somewhere, the Archive scribbled that down under "quotable nonsense that sounds wise."

The security guard held up a hand.

"Next," he called. "Candidate card."

I stepped forward, handed over my civic ID and printed interview call letter. The scanner flickered. My name and track appeared briefly on his holo-glasses.

"Ambassador Studies," he read. His tone didn't change, but something in the way he nodded did.

"Second floor," he said. "Hall A-3. Panels running in order. Follow the signs."

He handed my card back.

Rao stopped at the glass line marked "Candidates Only."

"Go," he said.

"See you after," I said.

The waiting area outside Hall A-3 was a battleground of different kind.

Some candidates sat rigid, backs straight, eyes closed, lips moving silently around prepared answers. Others flipped through last-minute notes on tablets, scrolling faster than any human brain could assimilate. A few just stared at the floor, perhaps hoping it would open and swallow them before their name was called.

"C-17?" an usher called.

A girl near the front stood, wiped her palms against her skirt, and disappeared into the hall.

"C-18?"

Another boy. Another disappearance.

My own number—C-23—approached at the speed of inevitability.

[Heart Rate: +24% above baseline, the Archive noted. Breath Pattern: stable]

"Let me handle the panic," it said. "You handle the sentences."

"I thought you refused to assist in exams," I thought back.

[Interviews are not exams,] it replied. [They are theatre. Different clause in my contract.]

"Good," I whispered.

"C-23?" the usher called.

I stood.

The walk from the waiting bench to the interview room door felt like the longest twenty steps in my life so far, which was impressive considering I'd once done an entire bridge on failing lungs.

The door slid open with efficient quiet.

Inside, the room was colder.

A long table at the far end. Three people seated behind it.

On the left, a woman in Institute's formal—a dark blazer with the Ambassador Studies sigil at the lapel, hair pulled back, eyes sharp and assessing.

In the middle, a man with the neutral, polished look of someone who had spent a lot of time in rooms like this and had learned how to take up exactly as much space as he wanted and no more.

On the right, a broad-shouldered, black-haired man with grey strands peeking in between, who wore a suit that fit like it had never known the word "off-the-rack." His beard was trimmed short, his gaze heavy. A Zarya sun pin glowed at his collar.

The pressure in the room shifted a fraction when my eyes met his.

[Chairman], the Archive whispered. [Probability: 93%].

Of course.

"Good morning," I said, focusing on the space between their eyebrows because Rao had told me to. "My name is Arin Valen."

"Sit, Mr. Valen," the woman on the left said. Her voice was clear, clipped. "I'm Instructor Diksha Sen, Ambassador Studies.

This is Mr. Kenzo Hayato , external observer . And this is Chairman Mikhail Orlov Volkonskaya of the Zarya Consortium."

"Good morning," I repeated, sitting carefully. Feet flat. Back straight. Hands resting lightly on my thighs.

"Your file is… interesting," Mr. Hayato said, glancing at his terminal. "Vasantpur resident. No special ability certification. You did not even attend Middle school properly and yet you have High written scores."

There was no judgment in his tone. Yet.

"Thank you, sir," I said, because that seemed safer than "My lungs and I appreciate your interest."

"So," Instructor Sen said, folding her hands. "Why Ambassador Studies?"

This was one of the questions I had rehearsed the most. Which, as it turned out, was exactly the problem.

I inhaled.

"Ma'am," I began, "Ambassador Studies sits at the intersection of geopolitics, culture, and ethics. I believe that in a post-Fog world, the primary battleground is no longer just physical territory, but narrative space. Whichever civilization can control the framing of interactions—"

"Stop," a small, sane part of my brain said.

"—will have disproportionate influence over treaty structures and long-term resource allocation," I finished.

Instructor Sen blinked once.

"So," she said, "you want to control narratives?"

"Not control," I said quickly. "Facilitate. Mediate between competing narratives to arrive at stable equilibria that—"

"—that you deem acceptable," Chairman Orlov finished for me, his accent wrapping itself around the words.

His eyes were on me now. They were not friendly. They weren't hostile either. They were weighing.

I realized, belatedly, that I sounded less like a thirteen-year-old from a factory district and more like a tired think-tank report.

"Next question," Mr. Hayato said smoothly, perhaps for my sake.

"Mr. Valen," he said, "there has been a debate recently about whether the Institute should reserve additional seats for candidates without blessings or guild backing. You have neither. Do you think such reservations are fair?"

I knew this one. I'd thought through it with the Archive two nights ago.

"Yes, sir," I said. "I believe they are fair as transitional measures to correct existing structural imbalances in access to training and opportunities. Without them, the Academy risks becoming an echo chamber for guild narratives, which weakens—"

"You are answering like a manifesto," Instructor Sen cut in, not unkindly. "Try again. One sentence. No jargon."

I swallowed.

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "I think it's fair to give people like me a chance to catch up, so that when we stand next to guild candidates, the only difference between us is what we do, not where we started."

That landed better. I saw something soften, just a fraction, at the corner of her mouth.

Chairman Orlov's gaze did not soften.

"And yet," he said, his voice heavy, "if we fill this Institute with weak bodies for the sake of fairness, who will stand at the Frontlines when things that come through that fog do not care about fairness?"

There was no good one-line answer to that.

"Those who can," I said softly. "This track doesn't send people to hold the front line with their bodies, sir. It sends them to hold it with their words. A healthy fool in this chair would do more damage than a sick person who understands consequences."

My hands had clenched on my thighs without my noticing.

Instructor Sen's eyes sharpened.

"So you," she said, "are not a fool."

"I try not to be, ma'am," I said.

"Sometimes try is not enough," she said. "Next question. Describe a time you changed your mind about something important."

My rehearsed answer was ready.

"I once believed that merit alone was enough to—"

"Boring," Chairman Orlov said flatly.

I blinked.

"Sir?"

"Every candidate we have seen says some version of that," he said. "Merit. Hard work. Gated world, blah blah. Tell me something that is not in your notes."

My mouth went dry.

"I—"

The room tilted, just a little. Not from health this time. From the realization that I had walked in here wearing a version of myself I thought they wanted to see: composed, clever, shaped by exam prep and Rao's drills.

And they were already peeling it off.

"Permission to… get some air, sir?" I heard myself say.

Three sets of eyebrows went up. Behind them, three slightly different calculations flickered.

"You want a break?" Mr. Hayato said.

"Yes, sir," I said. "For… one minute. I won't open any notes. I just… need to reset."

It was a risk. Panels were not fond of disruptions.

Instructor Sen looked at the others.

"We are not in a rush. Let the boy breathe" she said quietly.

Chairman Orlov studied me for a moment longer, then inclined his head, once.

"One minute," he said. "Do not run away."
