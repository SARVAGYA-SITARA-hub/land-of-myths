Interview - III

1,961 words
Reading on

The door clicked softly behind Arin as he stepped out, leaving the interview hall suddenly larger and much quieter than it had been a while ago.

For a few seconds, none of the three people at the panel's table moved.

Instructor Diksha Sen was the first to exhale. She set her pen down,

"Unusual," she said. "Most of them try to bleed to death on the carpet before admitting they need a pause."

Kenzo Hayato's mouth curved, almost but not quite into a smile. The Japanese Ambassador sat with his back straight and his hands loosely folded.

"He asked before he shattered," Hayato said. "That, in itself, is data."

Mikhail Orlov Volkonsky did not immediately respond. The Zarya Consortium representative leaned back in his chair, large hands folded over a chest still thick with the kind of muscle that did not come from office gyms.

"Data," Orlov repeated, his accent flattening the word. "What I see is a child who treats an interview like a stage and then asks for an intermission when he forgets his lines."

Sen's eyes flicked to him. "You think that's all this is?"

"I think," Orlov said, "we already heard the real problem when he opened his mouth."

He reached for the sheet in front of him, the compact dossier the Institute had prepared for each candidate. Civic ID. Medical summary. Academic history. A single line under "Affiliations": None. No guild, no sponsorship, no blessing certification.

"Listen again in your head," he went on, and his voice shifted into a dry imitation of Arin's earlier answer. "'Ambassador Studies sits at the intersection of geopolitics, culture, and ethics. Primary battleground is narrative space.'" He snorted. "He's thirteen. Not a policy brief."

Hayato glanced down at his own notes, where the same words sat underlined twice. He did not deny the accuracy of the impression.

"He has read too much and lived too little," Orlov said. "He speaks in downloaded phrases. The children now—" He broke off, searching for the word, then found it. "Perform. Always performing. Every room is an audition.

Sen turned her pen once between her fingers. "That is not entirely their fault," she said. "We built the rooms. We set the auditions."

"We built training grounds," Orlov retorted. "They turned them into theatres."

He tapped the dossier with one blunt finger. "Look at his answers. On reservations, on fairness. He knows the arguments. He arranges them nicely, like plastic soldiers on a shelf. But when I ask him something that isn't in his notes—'tell me a time you changed your mind'—he freezes like someone turned off the teleprompter."

"Because you cut him off mid-setup," Hayato pointed out mildly. "You have a talent for kicking away ladders, Orlov-san."

"If a boy can only stand on a ladder, he should not be on this track," Orlov said. "We are not recruiting parrots. We are recruiting people who will stand between this country and whatever walks out of that Fog.

Sen's expression did not change, but the tip of her pen paused above the paper.

"Every year it gets worse. These candidates—they've all watched the same tutorials, read the same 'How to Impress a Panel' guides." He lifted his hands in a small, irritated gesture. "They talk about 'leadership' and 'narrative' and 'impact metrics.' Ask them what frightens them and you will get a line about 'failing my potential' as if they are pitching to investors, not talking to human beings."

Hayato's eyes warmed with a trace of rueful amusement.

"In my country," he said, "we have the same problem. Different words. Same performance."

Orlov grunted. "There you are," he said. "This is what they think we want. A polished mask. A rehearsed humility. 'I believe in fairness; I believe in narrative equilibria.'" He mimicked Arin's cadence just enough to make the resemblance sting. "But when I press—when I say, 'You are weak; what then?'—you saw it. The script ran out."

Sen's gaze dropped briefly to the medical note in Arin's file: terminal pulmonary degeneration, stabilised, functional capacity improved but fragile. Body unsuitable for strenuous frontline operations without risk.

"The script ran out," she agreed quietly. "The boy did not."

Orlov looked at her, irritation pausing long enough to let interest through.

"Explain," he said.

"He could have defended himself with anger," Sen said. "Or collapsed into self-pity. He did neither. When you talked about weak bodies filling the Institute, he did not cry about fairness. He said, 'Those who can will stand at the Frontlines. This track holds the line with words.'" She tapped her notes. "Then he added, 'A healthy fool would do more damage than a sick person who understands consequences.' That was not from any coaching manual I've seen."

"That line was… unexpected," Hayato conceded. "Not elegant. But honest."

"Even fools can learn a good sentence," he said, but the edge of denial had dulled.

"Fools don't usually distinguish between front line and negotiating table," Hayato said. "He does, He knows he is not a spear. He is not trying to pretend otherwise. That already puts him ahead of half the guild progeny we see."

There was a small silence. Somewhere outside the hall, the muffled murmur of other candidates and ushers drifted past, a reminder that time was still moving.

Sen glanced at the small clock projected in the corner of her tablet.

"Forty seconds," she said. "He will be back soon."

Orlov snorted. "If he has not run away."

"Do you honestly think he will?" Hayato asked.

Orlov hesitated. The image rose unbidden—thin shoulders held too straight in a borrowed shirt, the stubborn line of a jaw that had heard too many versions of "no" and learned to keep walking anyway. The way the boy had asked for a minute had not been the plea of someone trying to escape; it had been more like a sailor requesting a breath before diving again.

"No," he admitted grudgingly. "He will come back. That is not my complaint."

"Then what is?" Sen asked.

He looked down at his own notes. Under "Strengths" someone—probably the Institute staffer who'd compiled the initial file—had written: exceptional theoretical grasp; adaptive reasoning; high persistence index despite health constraints. Under "Concerns": physical fragility; tendency to over-intellectualise; possible overreliance on rehearsed frameworks.

"The boy has spine," Orlov said. "I am not blind. I have commanded people with less. But spirit is not rare. We can find spirit in any district if we push hard enough. What is becoming rare is the ability to exist unperformed."

Sen's brow creased.

"Unperformed?" she repeated.

"Yes," Orlov said. "To be what you are without asking, 'How does this look?' All these children really think about is the gaze. Their parents'. Their guild's. Social feeds. Rankings. They learn to know how to appear long before they learn how to be."

He spread his hands slightly. "So we ask, 'Why this track?' And instead of an answer, we get a branding statement."

Hayato's gaze turned inward, as if replaying the earlier exchange in his own mind.

"'Primary battleground is narrative space,'" he said softly. "Yes. That did sound like a branding statement."

Sen did not argue.

"I am not surprised," Orlov said. "I am tired."

It came out sharper than he intended. For a moment, the room held not an old soldier in a polished suit but a man who had watched too many young faces disappear behind armour, physical or otherwise.

Hayato saw the flicker and, as always, pretended not to.

"Let us be precise," Hayato said instead. "What did today's performance tell us? Not about 'this generation' in the abstract. About this boy."

Sen straightened, grateful for the shift back into clear lines.

"His knowledge is solid," she said. "Not exceptional in raw volume—we have seen guild candidates who can recite resource treaties clauses in their sleep—but his connections between topics are better than average. When I asked him to remove jargon, he did so on the second attempt, not the fifth."

"He listens," Hayato added. "When corrected, he adjusts without becoming defensive.

"He also folds under an unprepared question," Orlov said. "You saw his face when I asked about changing his mind. He reached for the safe story—'I used to believe merit was enough'—the one every poor candidate keeps in their pocket. When I took it away, he froze."

"Or he realised it would come out false," Sen said quietly. "And stopped."

Orlov's eyes sharpened. "You give him much credit."

"I give him the credit his file demands," she replied. "Look." She tapped another section of the dossier, the one summarising the medical reports from Vasantpur: left lung functional at thirty-one percent a few months prior, right lobe overcompensating, prognosis 'one year, possibly more' with rest and minimal exertion. Then, the later update from the local physician: structural strain decreased; capacity improved; daily ambulation now possible; cause attributed to 'unorthodox but effective breathing regimen.'

"I have seen no thirteen year old who would design a training routine for an organ that is trying to kill them," Sen said. "Fewer would stick to it. He did. That tells me his mind has changed about something quite important already. It used to think, 'I am dying.' Now it thinks, 'I might be late, but I can still arrive.'"

Hayato's gaze softened.

"And yet, when asked for a story, he reached for theory," he said. "That mismatch is… interesting."

"Interesting is a polite word," Orlov muttered.

He leaned back further, chair creaking softly.

"I understand your point," he said grudgingly. "But do you understand mine? This… boy, and others like him, they have been taught that what matters is how they present their suffering, not how they survive it. So when asked, 'Who are you?' they do not think of bridges in the rain or lungs on fire. They think, 'What is the correct narrative?' It makes them brittle."

He looked towards the door again. "What happens when the other side does not care for their narrative?"

Sen followed his gaze.

"If he were applying for Guardian command or Combat," she said, "I would agree. We do not send brittle bodies or brittle selves to the front line. But Ambassador Studies is another kind of front. Here, we need people who can see the performance, because they will swim in it every day."

"Seeing performance is not the same as being trapped in your own," Orlov said.

"True," Hayato said. "Which is why this one minute is useful. You forced him off the prepared path. Now we will see what he brings back. A new mask, or a person."

Orlov gave him a sidelong look.

"You think a single minute will do that?"

"I have sat in enough negotiation rooms to know that the ten seconds after a person loses their script are often the only honest ones you get," Hayato replied. "He did something rare for his age group. He admitted he needed to reset instead of faking through. That tells me he has at least some instinct for not lying to himself."

"Or he just did not want to embarrass himself in front of us," Orlov said.

Hayato smiled faintly. "Those are not mutually exclusive."

The clock on Sen's tablet chimed softly.

"One minute," she said. "Time."

As if summoned by the word, the door handle moved.

All three panelists fell silent. Whatever else they thought about the boy, each of them knew the small ceremony of these seconds mattered. Candidates might remember only fragments of what they themselves said in interviews; they almost always remembered how they were watched.

The door opened.
