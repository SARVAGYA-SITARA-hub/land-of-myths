Six Months

1,520 words
Reading on

The library smelled different at six in the morning.

Less like people, more like dust and old paper slowly waking up.

"Tables," said Megha, like always.

"Tables," I repeated.

Reading Hall A was a graveyard of yesterday's ambitions. Open books abandoned mid-paragraph. Empty cups with the brown ring of last tea clinging to their sides. Scribbled notes on loose sheets that might once have been important and were now just… paper.

I put the tub down, picked up the rag, and began.

Wipe. Stack. Clear. Breathe.

By now, the sequence moved through me without needing supervision. On the inhale, the Null Frame rose automatically around my left lung, a quiet scaffold of structured absence holding frayed tissue in place. On the exhale, it dissolved just as automatically.

[Null Craft – Mastery: 99%], the Archive noted in the corner of my vision. [Natural progress for any idiot who insists on abusing a fragment art as a respiratory aid.]

I wiped, stacked, cleared.

By eight, the first wave of aspirants arrived.

They came in clusters—study groups formed in coaching centers, wearing different brands of the same uniform: decent shoes, holo-tablets, noise-cancelling earpieces hooked around their necks like status symbols. Their bags looked heavier than my entire wardrobe.

"Bro, my dad said if I clear General Track for Engineering and Logistics on first attempt, he'll sponsor a Europe-tour package during vacation before opening," one of them said once, sliding into a seat as I wiped the neighboring table.

"Please," the other scoffed. "My mom said if I get top hundred, she'll talk to our guild contact for an internal recommendation. Guardian stream, General track."

Their voices blurred into background noise as I moved past, tub rattling softly.

I wasn't jealous, Not exactly.

Envy is too simple a word for the feeling of watching people start a race from ten meters ahead, with better shoes and fresh lungs, while you're still negotiating with your own ribcage.

Besides, I reminded myself, I had something they didn't.

I had already failed once.

UPSC Mains. Rain. Headlights. The memory was a scar I could poke when I needed perspective. These kids had pressure. I had… precedent.

"Focus," I muttered under my breath.

[Focus] the Archive agreed. [On cleaning, Your true calling.]

I rolled my eyes and wiped another table.

The ache in my arms came first—muscles protesting repeated motion after years of being used only for lifting spoons. Then the heaviness in my chest

[Vital Capacity: 44% → 45%], the Archive logged one day

[Coughing frequency: down 56% compared to baseline month.]

I leaned against a pillar for a moment.

"You okay, kid?" Megha called from the front desk.

"Break," I said. "Then more tables."

I sat on a chair by the wall, watching the reading hall fill. Students slid into habitual seats. Some spread out across entire tables like minor nobility surveying their fiefdoms. Others hunched protectively over borrowed screens.

On good days, when my hands were steady and my lungs weren't chewing on rust, I'd pick up a crumpled revision sheet someone had left behind—topics highlighted in neon, questions partially solved. I'd smooth it out carefully and tuck it between the pages of my own compendium.

On bad days, I just breathed and counted the seconds between coughs.

Those days grew fewer.

Lunch was the best part of the day.

Not because of the food—though that mattered—but because it came with my mother.

She'd appear at the security gate around one-thirty, dupatta pulled tight against the outside dust, tiffin in hand. The guard would wave her through with a nod now; they knew each other by shift-timing association.

"Ma's here," Megha would call across the lobby, and I'd emerge from between two shelves, dust on my fingers, shirt damp at the back.

"Sit," Ma would say

"You vanish into these books and forget you have a stomach."

Rice. Dal. Sometimes a bit of sabzi if the mess had been generous. Once in a rare while.

"You look less bone today," she'd say, watching me eat.

"More… person."

It was not a medical term, but it wasn't wrong.

[Weight: +10.3 kg since baseline, the Archive confirmed. Caloric absorption improving. Cough intensity trending downward.]

I ate slowly, letting each mouthful sit for a moment before swallowing.

Some days, when the tiffin was almost empty, a shadow would fall across our corner.

"Mrs. Valen," Rao's voice would say. "You brought the boy fuel again, I see."

Ma would stand up so fast her chair scraped. "Namaste, sir."

"At ease," he'd say, amusement tucked into the corners of his mouth. "This is not a parade ground."

He'd set down a small container on the table. Banana slices. Apple wedges. Once, I swear, two pieces of an actual orange, glowing like small suns.

"My wife has decided we are both too old to finish all the fruit she sends," he'd say. "It would be a tragedy to waste it."

"It's too much," Ma would protest.

"It is arithmetic," Rao would reply. "If two old people cannot finish a kilo of fruit, but two old people and one stubborn boy can, the solution is obvious."

He'd leave before either of us could argue further.

Afternoons belonged to paper.

After cleaning duty, I'd move to a corner table in Reading Hall B with my compendium, the "found" sheets, and—more recently—an almost-new notebook that had somehow appeared in my bag.

I found it wedged inside my bag

[I see the spirits of surplus stationary have taken a liking to you] the Archive observed.

"These just… show up," I said, turning the pen over. "Conveniently at the start of a new topic."

[Yes] it said. [Mysterious.]

I glanced toward the glass wall of Rao's office. He was bent over a terminal, frowning at a report.

"Very mysterious," I agreed.

I opened the notebook.

Unlike my previous life, I didn't copy everything. Years of UPSC coaching had taught me the trap of writing Laxmikanth into five different registers and calling it progress.

Now, my notes were about structure.

Left page: topics and subtopics.

Right page: questions.

Ambassador Studies & Delegation – Outline

Fog Phenomenology and Energy Graph Analysis – Basic and Applied

Foreign Civilizations – Political structures, cultural norms

Geopolitics – Resource lines, guild alliances, Resource control

Economics – Cross-Continental trade, tariffs, reparations

Ethics & Protocol – First-contact doctrine, treaties, negotiation frameworks

Aptitude – Data interpretation, logic, decision-making under constraint

Old habits die harder than people.

I made a table for the week: Days on one axis, topics on the other.

Morning: cleaning and passive listening to other students' group discussions. Afternoon: one major topic plus practice questions.

Evening: revision and mapwork—both literal maps and mind-maps.

[You are treating your life as a multi-paper exam], the Archive said.

"It worked better than treating it as a story," I replied.

Sometimes, in the late afternoon, Rao would wander past my table with the aimless precision of someone who definitely hadn't come there by accident.

Evenings belonged to revision.

By six, the study groups started thinning. Parents called. Coaching sessions resumed.

I'd stack my notes, close my compendium, and spend an hour just… going over.

No writing. Just tracing lines in my head.

UPSC had taught me that revision isn't just rereading; it's rebuilding. You take apart a chapter and put it back together in your own language.

While my mind rearranged information, my body rearranged breath.

One evening, as I was halfway through silently reciting the key points of Gate Ethics, the Archive cleared its metaphorical throat.

[Null Craft – Mastery: 100%].

Passive condition satisfied: Cycle of Breath-Frame-Release now integrated into autonomic patterns.

I blinked.

"So the frame just… runs?" I asked internally.

[Within limits], the Archive replied. [Your body has adopted the pattern. When strain markers rise above a certain threshold, the art triggers without conscious input.]

"So now I can climb library stairs with ease?" I suggested.

[Those remain your natural predator], it replied.

Still, numbers didn't lie.

[Vital Capacity: 48%.]

[Cough frequency: down 59%.]

[Weight: +11.2 kg since baseline.]

Even Ma had noticed.

"You cough less now," she said one night as we sat in our room, fan ticking above.

"Not scaring the neighbours as often."

"Building local goodwill," I said. "Very important for Ambassador track."

She shook her head. "Your jokes are getting worse. That's how I know you feel better."

I smiled into my cup of tea.

Time doesn't speed up when you're busy.

It just stops warning you that it's leaving.

Days blurred,

Morning: arrive with Megha. Clean. Listen.

Late morning: breathe. Survive.

Afternoon: notes. "Found" mock papers slid between compendium pages that were, somehow, always the next topics I needed.

"How strange," I said, making sure to say it loud enough that Rao, passing by, could hear and not-smile.

Evening: revision. Silent recitations. Occasional whispered arguments with the Archive about whether Ambassador Studies would actually take a half-dead thirteen-year-old with no visible importance.

Night: home. Dinner. Ma's running commentary on her day at the factory. My filtered version of my day at the library.

Six months passed like that.

And then the Notification came.
