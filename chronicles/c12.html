The Day - I

1,186 words

For a while, there was only darkness, the ticking ceiling fan, and the soft, measured drag of my own breath.

then the first ray of the sun spilled through my window

Then the Stellar Archive lit up at the edge of my vision.

[Institute Written Examination – Day 0.]

[Body Status: Functional. Anxiety Levels: Predictably Elevated.]

"Shut up," I whispered.

I lay there for one more breath. My heart was already tapping out a map of the exam hall that didn't exist yet.

On the narrow table by the stove, Ma's old, cracked phone chimed the hour.

5:00 AM.

I swung my legs off the bed and sat up.

Rao had given me two days leave.

"Nothing productive happens in your head in the last forty-eight hours before an exam except panic," he'd said, leaning back in his chair two days earlier.

Megha had nodded in agreement.

"Sir, that sounds like personal experience," I'd said.

"Decades of it," he'd replied. "Go home. Revise lightly. Sleep. If I see you in this building before the exam, I'll have you thrown out for loitering."

So I'd stayed home. Revised with a light hand instead of tearing through new material.

Now, on the morning that counted, the room felt smaller than it had ever been.

Ma was already up. She moved around the stove with the controlled urgency.

"Brush, then tea," she said without turning.

By six, I was dressed.

Grey T-shirt, the newer one with fewer repairs. Dark trousers that had been ironed into a temporary truce with the concept of straight lines. The shoes I'd cleaned last night until the original color rediscovered itself.

Ma set up my collar anyway.

"I am going to write, not attend a wedding," I said.

"You're going to change your life," she snapped back. "Let me fix your collar."

She stepped back, surveyed me with a critical eye, and made a small, satisfied sound.

On the table, she'd packed a small bag: water bottle, tiffin, an extra handkerchief, and the exam admit card folded inside a transparent sleeve like it was a relic.

She handed the card to me.

"Check," she said.

**Name: Arin Valen.**

Track: Ambassador Studies & Delegation – Candidate (Written).

Center: Vasantpur Municipal Complex – Block C.

Reporting time: 8:00 AM.

Exam: 9:00 AM – 12:00 PM, 1:00 PM – 4:00 PM.

"If we leave now, we'll reach by seven-thirty," Ma said. "I don't trust buses on days that matter."

"We barely trust them on days that don't," I said.

She ignored that and picked up her dupatta.

"Let's go," she said.

The Archive slid a tiny update into view.

[Distance to Center: 7.1 km.]

[Recommended: Public Transit + Short Walk. Probability of "dramatic collapse en route": 18%.]

"We're taking the bus," I told it silently. "Not swimming the river."

[If you faint today, I will disown you as a Host.]

"Motivating," I murmured, and followed my mother.

The streets were different at dawn.

Less noise

We boarded a bus that smell of rust. Ma kept one hand on the rail, one lightly on my shoulder whenever the driver braked too hard. I sat by the window.

The municipal complex rose out of the morning haze like someone had taken all the seriousness in the city and poured it into one concrete block. High walls. Security scanners at the gate. Temporary banners hung across the entrance:

INSTITUTE WRITTEN EXAMINATION CENTER – VASANTPUR

MUNICIPAL COMPLEX.

Clusters of parents. Guild representatives in understated expensive clothing. Kids my age and older, some in proper uniforms, some in whatever passed for clean at their homes.

Ma tightened her hand on my shoulder.

"All these people…" she murmured.

[All these competitors] the Archive corrected.

We joined the slowly forming line at the main gate.

Ahead of us, a boy in a neat blazer leaned against his mother's side, scrolling lazily through a holo-tablet.

Next to them, another kid grinned at a man in an expensive-insignia overcoat.

"Dad, I'm telling you, I'm going to get into Combat Arts," he said.

"Let's clear the written before you start killing Fog-beasts," his father said dryly. "But yes. If you get Combat, I'll upgrade your gear. Full-set armour, not this old stuff."

Behind us, two girls whispered to each other, both clutching thin tablets.

"What if you get Gate Research?" the other teased. "You'll end up married to data in a basement."

"Better than some of the boys in our class," she shot back.

Ma watched them, her face carefully neutral.

She had nothing to offer me except a tiffin and a hand on my back.

Which, in a world full of representative cards and guild contacts, somehow felt like both not enough and more than most people ever get.

"You can still come home," she said quietly, as if she'd heard my thoughts. "If it doesn't work. We'll… manage."

I looked at her.

"If it doesn't work," I said, "we'll try again. And if that doesn't work, we'll find something else. But today, I'm not planning for 'if it doesn't work.'"

"Fine," she said.

Check-in was predictably humiliating.

One line for metal detection. Another for biometrics. A third for hall assignments. Each step accompanied by officials with tired eyes and voices worn smooth by repetition.

"Admit card."

"Thumb on the scanner."

"Hall C-2. Ground floor."

Ma walked with me until the "Parents Stop Here" line painted on the concrete.

Beyond it, the corridor led into exam halls. This side of the line, parents paced, prayed, argued with each other about cut-offs and quotas.

Ma said. "I'll wait at the tea stall outside."

"You don't have to wait all day," I said. "It's six hours, Ma. You have work—"

"I told the supervisor I have a medical thing," she said

Her hand came up, smoothed my hair automatically, then dropped.

"Write properly," she said.

I nodded, swallowed my saliva and crossed the gate

The exam hall was big enough to host a minor war.

Rows of single desks stretched from door to far wall, each with a candidate number stuck to the front. A clock hung high near the ceiling.

"Find your roll number," an invigilator barked.

I did.

C-2-173.

Middle row, slightly to the left.

I sat. Placed my pen parallel to the admit card.

Around me, the atmosphere thickened. The low buzz of whispering aspirants faded as more invigilators entered, carrying sachel-cases that rattled faintly with question papers.

[Heart Rate: +18% above resting.]

[This is not a life-and-death battle. You may relax your fight-or-flight response.]

"Exams killed me last time," I reminded it.

The chief invigilator took position by the desk at the front.

"Phones and devices are to remain in the lockers. Holo-wear off. Any active interface during the exam will result in disqualification."

Several kids with expensive earpieces shifted uncomfortably going out.

[I will not assist,] it said. [You are on your own in there.]

"Good," I thought. "This part is mine."

The papers were distributed.

Face down, a thin rectangle of doom.

"Do not turn the paper until instructed," the invigilator barked.

The seconds stretched.

Then: "Begin."

I flipped over..
