The World and the Water

2,002 words
Reading on

{Arin's POV}

It came. I moved before the thought arrived — weight on my left toe, heel kicked back, feet flipped under me. Then I turned, and dropped. The air blurred through where my chest had been a moment ago, and the stable came apart. Not smashed. Cut — a line through the wood and the horses.

“Kghh” I exhaled forcefully through my mouth.

‘I would have been severed in half had that connected’ I back flipped putting some difference between us.

He came through the opening he had made. Short. Neat. Gloves indoors. A bluish glow faded off his skin like heat off stone. The air around him moved.

"Tarkhan," a woman's voice came from behind him. "You have made it a public matter now."

My eyes widened hearing that name

‘Supreme commander Tarkhan?’ I wished for such a scenario to just be my misunderstanding. Yet it was shattered the next second.

She came through next. Red coat, two curved swords worn low and crossed.

Seeing her I remembered a name from the previous conversation

“Sellen Orath?” I spoke to myself mulling and finding a way to escape.

She turned her eyes towards me the moment I muttered her name and smiled.

"The city is sealed," she said. Pleasant voice. "You are not going anywhere." She said it like the ending had already been written.

Behind Tarkhan, seven men stepped onto the cobbles. I turned.

I turned, those seven men stood exactly one foot from each other. Draped in deep violet from head to toe. I couldn’t see their faces as it was covered by their hood.

I turned my gaze back to Sellen who took out the curved swords from her waist.

“Stay out of my hunt old man” she warned Tarkhan as she disappeared from her spot reappearing in front of me in split of a second.

*

The world dissolved partially as the time slowed, I saw her sword coming in an arc.

I closed in and tried to incapacitate her with a full fledged blow on solar plexus but she kicked off the ground and her knee slammed straight into my jaw.

Crack

Excruciating pain flared and my teeth rattled. But I managed to get hold of her leg.

I turned around to slam her into the ground

[Curse of Despair is Active]

[-1% Sanity]

I had one plan: get out. I was not there to win. She cut the air where I had been. I opened a null four feet left and she was already turning before I arrived. I opened one past the gate and she cut that too — not at the hole, at the air on the far side, where anything coming through would have to be. She had read the trick before I finished using it.

So I used the holes to hide behind. A null between us, me moving along the rubble behind the noise. She kicked through it. One under a trough, and I rolled; she stepped over the trough.

Every move I had, she had an answer that cost her nothing. Never overcommitted, never gave me the half-second a man needs to make a mistake look like luck. Five days I had lived on other people's mistakes. Tonight nobody was making any.

Then she came at me properly — not a probe, a decision.

She was stronger than me. Not a little. The first exchange put me on one knee; the second took the sword out of my hand into the wall; the third put her boot in my chest and I went back into the trough hard enough to lose the last of my air.

I got up. She let me, which was worse than if she had not — it meant she was not worried. I swung at her with my fist because it was the only weapon left, and she caught my wrist, turned it, and put me down again.

*

[Sanity: -2%]

The fear did not go away. It went quiet, and under it something opened. The colour drained out of the world at the edges. My heart went slow. And the thinking — the part that had kept me alive for five days — did not stop so much as get put aside, the way you put aside a coat when the work gets heavy.

I came off the ground at her.

*

She caught the first blow and it moved her. That was all — it moved her, and she looked at me with an expression I had not seen on her all night: surprise, and behind it, arithmetic. Then she stopped being careful.

It was still not close. She put two cuts on me — forearm, ribs — and neither landed on a man who had moved. I had not moved. I had eaten them to stay in reach. She hit me with everything; I came back from worse positions each time. I hit her in the mouth. She broke my nose, and I got a hand in her coat and pulled her into the wall.

This is the part I cannot explain. Nothing hurt. My body was a thing I was using, and the more it broke the less it argued.

She needed help. The Crimson Gale needed help, and I found that funny in a way I could not have explained.

*

The Supreme Commander walked into the yard and put himself in front of me. He did not hurry. The stones under his feet came apart as he came, and the ground where I stood began to go thin and grey.

I threw myself at him anyway.

Null under his feet. Nothing. Null behind his head — he had already moved. My fist at his throat and he was not there and then he was, and the back of his hand took me across the yard into the gate. I got up.

I put a hole in the air, went through it, came out behind him, and he had turned before I arrived — he had watched me do it four times and stopped needing to watch.

I put another and another. Twenty seconds each, then gone. Six, seven, each further into the yard with fewer places left. Somewhere in the eighth my hands stopped working and my knee stopped taking orders. I fell, got up out of pure refusal, and he broke my arm.

I did not feel it. That is the truth and it is not a boast. I looked down, understood the shape was wrong, and hit him with the other one.

*

He raised the sword the way a man raises an axe at a fallen tree. There was no room left behind me, and no hole I could open that would be finished before it landed.

The sword was in the air in front of my face. I could see the line of it, the light on the edge, a notch three inches up from the tip, and its shadow by my head.

And the world stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. The sword hung and did not move. The dust did not move. Her red coat did not.

And I could see.

Dots of golden light. Hundreds, then thousands, through the air in every direction, sitting in no order at all. Some close enough to touch, some specks of dust in sunlight. None of them moving. And every one joined to the others by a thin gold line.

The lines crossed and met other dots and crossed again, and the whole of it hung in the stopped air like a net thrown over the world — through the stable, the wall, him, me.

I did not know what they were. I do not know now. There was one behind Tarkhan, three feet from his shoulder blade.

I reached.

*

The world started again. I was standing behind him.

His sword finished its swing through the air where I had been and took the ground with it. He came out of it turning — a man who had hit nothing and knew what that meant — and found me behind his shoulder.

His face changed. Not fear. Worse. The face of a man doing sums he could not finish.

"You moved," he said.

I said nothing. My hands shook and something ran out of my nose. The woman had stopped dead, both swords half raised. The seven hoods turned at once, the way a flock of birds turns, looking at me.

"He moved," she said, very quietly.

*

The second from the end of the row lifted one hand. No sound, no wind, no flash. I was on my knees, arms pulled behind me by nothing, held flat the way a nail is held by a plank. My lungs stopped obeying me. I reached for the craft, and the door opened onto a wall.

I fought the word four times and got nothing, and on the fifth understood there was nothing to fight.

It is not a binding, I thought, swaying. It is a word. They said a word, and the world agreed with them.

Then a sound at the far end of the yard. Boots on wet stone. A head turning. Panak came around the end of the row into the lantern light.

*

They put me on my knees in the long hall, on the blue stone, under the imperial portrait with the crown's seal below it.

Panak had washed his face. Between sealing a city and taking apart a yard, he had found time to wash and put his coat on properly, and I do not know why that frightened me more than the wall.

"I did not kill her," I said.

"I did not kill anyone. I never went near the queen. I was in the passage with—" My voice came apart. "I never touched one person since I came to this country. Not one. Ask the soldiers. Ask the man who found the bodies. Please."

Please. It came out on its own. I had promised myself I would never be a man who says it, and I said it four times.

Panak listened. "I know," he said. The record-keeper's brush stopped.

"I know you did not kill her," he said again, and there was no kindness in it. He said it the way a man says the weather. "I knew before I left the palace. The wound was not made by a Parsharan blade. Every man here knows it, and the report I sent to the capital left that part out."

"Then why—"

"Because it does not matter what you did." He looked up at the portrait and said the rest to it. "I have spent my whole life kneeling to a crown that never looked down at my province. My queen is dead and the crown is laughing — laughing because I kneel on the floor of my own hall to a story about a Parsharan boy, because that is what the crown asked for, and I am doing it."

"This is not for the queen," he said. "She is past being served. Not for the crown either. This is for my city. If I hand the crown a dead Parsharan, the war does not come here. If I am too proud to do it, two hundred thousand people pay for my pride with their throats. And I am not a great man, boy, so I do not get to be proud."

"Put your head down."

My arms were held, my legs did not work, the craft was a locked door, and I had just begged a man in front of his guards — and I lifted my head and looked at him.

"I did not kill her," I said. Flat. Not a plea this time. I wanted it on the record.

"No," said Panak. "You did not."

Then he looked past me and said, in a voice that carried to the far end of the hall:

"Record: the prisoner did not confess. The prisoner did not confess at any point, and he is to be recorded as not having confessed."

The brush moved.
