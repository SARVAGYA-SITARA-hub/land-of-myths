Orientation

2,470 words
Reading on

The Institute had many ways to remind you you were small.

Last night, it had used stars and a martial dome with an open ceiling. Today, it used chairs.

Rows of them. Hundreds. All perfectly aligned in a descending arc in front of a stage that looked like it had been carved out of some retired war room.

I arrived late.

Not missing the speech late. Just late enough that every sane seat was gone, and the Archive had added a faintly judgemental tint to my Stat Window.

The sound in the hall was the sound of a hive trying to learn manners.

Students in the same dark blue uniforms, striped now with clean track colors—red flashes for Guardian and Combat, blue for Research, green for Engineering and Logistics, white for Ambassadorial—tested out their bands, their voices. Some of them sat straight, already rehearsing important person posture.

I scanned for empty chairs.

First row: full, obviously. Second: half full, but the remaining spaces were wedged between clusters of candidates who already knew each other well enough to lean in close and whisper.

Third to eighth: occupied by every variety of early, prepared, and annoyingly awake student.

Last row, against the back wall: three empty seats.

Two of them marked by bags and one at the far corner.

I took the corner.

The metal of the chair was cold. The view from here turned the hall into layers: heads, bands, track stripes, the pale stone of the front wall, and above it all the Institute crest—radiating lines over a stylized world map—etched into glass.

On the stage, two lecterns flanked a central emblem inlaid into the floor. Thin lines, crossing like constellations, traced an eight‑pointed pattern. At its center, a small crown and orbit motif caught the light—simple, but deliberate.

The noise level dropped gradually, the way it always does when enough people realize something official might be starting. Conversations folded themselves down as the hall lights shifted.

A side door at the edge of the stage opened.

He walked out like the room already belonged to him and he was just taking inventory.

Tall, shoulders set with the quiet precision of someone who had spent enough time in training domes to know exactly how much space his body occupied. His uniform had the same dark blue base as ours, but the stripes at his collar and sleeves were a deeper gold like the color of band of that girl last night

His wrist carried it more clearly.

“The Institute really enjoys labelling people,” I said under my breath.

[Institution enjoy order. You enjoy complaining about it.] Archive mocked me.

He took the central lectern. For a moment, he let his gaze move over us, row by row, like he was counting not bodies but variables.

Then he smiled. Small. Precise.

“Welcome to the Institut d’études Intermondiales,” he said.

His voice carried cleanly. “I’m Rian Sol Virel,” he went on. “Guardian track, House Aster, Third year. Some of you have already looked up my name in the Institute’s hierarchy lists. Some of you will pretend you haven’t. That will change.”

House Aster. That was new. I filed it.

“The first thing you need to understand,” Rian said, “is that this place is not a school that wants you to feel comfortable. It’s a fortress that wants you to become precise.”

He lifted his wrist so the band caught the overhead light.

“Look at your bands.”

Across the hall, sleeves shifted as people raised their hands. Red. Blue. Green. White. A few bands glowed brighter than others, edged in faint metallic halos—silver, gold.

I glanced at mine.

White. Ambassadorial. A clean ring.

“That color on your wrist,” Rian said, as if he’d heard the thought, “is your worth here. Track color. Quotas attached. It tells the Institute, at a glance, how much trust you’ve been given and how much you are expected to earn.”

He let the silence sit for a heartbeat.

“Those people whom you once admired, who once were called heroes, who on their life held back whatever was beyond that fog-“ he continued

“Every one of them sat where you sit now,” Rian continued. “Every one of them wore a band. Some started at the bottom of the hierarchy. Some started near the top. Legacy isn’t about where you begin, it’s about how much you can endure long enough to become.”

His eyes moved again. I felt, rather than saw, the moment they swept the last row.

Hierarchy exists for one reason, he said. “Not to decorate egos, but to keep chaos from eating purpose. Track colors say what you train for and margins you have.”

His band glowed briefly, gold ring bright.

“Silver fascia,” he said, “Faculty, researchers, House Masters or the people who agreed to burn their lives to nurture you . Gold marks apex hierarchy—students already carrying responsibilities heavier than most faculty had at their age. Crown quota—candidates whose seats exist because someone with jurisdiction over nations and consortiums demanded they be trained, fast.”

I tried not to look at my wrist. I failed.

“You don’t earn yellow,” Rian said. “You’re born with it. But you still have to justify it. The Crown doesn’t like wasted investments. The Institute likes them even less.”

Rian’s gaze swept back to the front rows.

“If your band is plain,” he said, “good. You’re baseline. You have room to climb. If your band is edged in silver, you’ve been given less room to fail. If it’s gold, you’re carrying not just your own story but someone else’s budget.”

A ripple of low laughter passed through the hall. It cut the tension without erasing it.

“Hierarchy here will press you,” he finished. “If you’re lower, you’re expected to climb. If you’re higher, you’re expected to justify every millimetre of height. That is how you honor the legacy of this place. That is how you become more than a name on a list.”

He stepped back from the lectern.

Applause started unevenly—front rows first, then spreading backward, gathering speed like gossip.

I clapped because not clapping made you stand out, and standing out in a room full of people with sharper lungs and better bands seemed statistically unwise.

The side door opened again.

This arrival did not come with orbit energy. It came with vector.

She walked out like she had already calculated the hall’s dimensions and decided exactly how much of them she needed.

Same dark blue uniform. Different details. Narrow red stripes ran along her shoulders and the edges of her sleeves—not Guardian track red, but a deeper, more formal tone. Her band was silver at the core, edged with a scarlet line.

The Archive pinged.

She moved to the second lectern. Adjusted the mic with precise fingers. Looked at us once, the way a doctor looks at test results before writing a prescription.

“Good morning,” she said. Her accent was British, English exact. “I’m Olivia Silev. Second‑year, Student Council, Discipline Committee. My job is to make sure you understand what you’re wearing and why you don’t get to pretend ignorance later.”

The hall settled. Whatever noise remained turned into the quiet shuffle of people sitting straighter.

“We’ll start with the obvious,” Olivia said. “Bands.”

She raised her wrist. The silver‑scarlet ring glinted under the lights.

“Red bands for Guardian and Combat. Blue for Research and Phenomenology. Green for Engineering and Logistics. White for Ambassadorial and support. Those colours are not fashion choices. They are your primary directions”

Panels unfolded in my vision: four simple diagrams, colours labelled.

“Red,” she said, “means you have chosen to learn how to stand at the edge of the Fog and not die. Blue means you will spend years understanding why the Fog behaves the way it does, and how reality fractures. Green means you will build and maintain the structures that make everyone else’s heroics survivable. White means you will sit across tables from entities who can erase cities and attempt, with words, not to let them.”

Her gaze skimmed the white‑band section. I felt it land on me for half a second. My lungs caught, then remembered how to function.

“Track colours can change,” Olivia said. “It’s rare, but possible. If you demonstrate aptitude in something your current track cannot use, the Institute will move you. Do not cling to color for ego. Cling to function.”

The diagram shifted. Rings appeared around the base colours—plain, silver, yellow, gold.

“Now Miscellanous,” she said. “Plain bands indicate standard intake. You passed the exam. You are here. Congratulations. Work.”

“Silver color indicates higher positions like Senior Guardians, lead researchers, logistics heads, ambassadors already carrying live responsibilities. You will know who they are long before you wish you didn’t.”

“Gold color is apex student hierarchy, Crown quota seats.”

She let the words sit.

“You may have seen them,” she said. “Bands clad in full gold. Those seats exist because the Crown, consortiums, or allied governments designated a certain number of candidates to be trained with priority access to Institute resources. They represent districts, alliances, lines of responsibility you will never be allowed to misplace.”

“If you don’t wear golden band,” Olivia continued, “do not waste energy resenting those who do. The Fog does not care what color of band you have. It eats without discrimination. Your job, regardless of color, is to become solid enough that when something tries to rewrite physics in front of you, you are part of the structure that holds.”

She paused. The hall was quiet enough that you could hear someone’s cough three rows ahead echo against the back wall.

“Bands can change,” she said. “Upwards and downwards. If you perform consistently”

Olivia’s tone adjusted—still firm, but less surgical.

“So what do you do with all this?” she asked. “Simple things. You show up to your classes. You train when scheduled. You rest when medically necessary instead of when boredom tells you to.”

“You ask for help before you’re drowning,” she said. “You push hard enough to grow, not hard enough to break. You remember that the Institute is not interested in how tragic your backstory is. It is interested in whether you can carry the weight it will put on your shoulders.”

Her hand moved. The diagrams folded back, replaced by four new sigils arranged around the crest.

“And because this place does not run on individuals alone,” she continued, “you will have to join one of any four Houses.”

The hall shifted. People sat up a little. Gossip had reached the dorms about Houses; this was the first official mention.

“House Aster,” Olivia said, indicating a sigil shaped like a starburst over a shield, “focuses on Guardian operations and strategic command. Combat, defence, front‑line decision‑making. If your red band makes you feel too quiet, Aster will give it volume.”

“House Leviathan,” a deep, swirling emblem like a coiled sea‑beast, “handles field deployment and heavy combat applications. Domes, Gate sorties, direct engagement with Fog events and rescue operations. They collect scars, disciplinary notes, and songs in approximately equal measure.”

“House Meridian,” a rising line over a horizon, “centres on research, scouting, phenomenology, and analytical support. They are the ones who keep everyone else from walking blindly into fracture zones because the readings looked ‘interesting’.”

“House Forge,” a stylised hammer over an orbit, “specialises in engineering, logistics, and innovation. Constructs, grids, the support systems that make the Institute more than walls and bravado. If something in this fortress works, there is probably a Forge student behind it.”

The sigils rotated slowly.

“You will each join one House,” Olivia said. “Not today, but soon. The Archive will generate preliminary recommendations based on your exams, your track, and your medical and psychological files. You will express preferences. Faculty will review. Final House assignment is a decision, not a vote.”

House point columns appeared next to each sigil, currently empty.

“Houses earn points,” she said. “Academic performance. Simulations. combat trial results. Innovation challenges. Inter‑House events. You will contribute, whether by being brilliant in class or by not crashing a training dome during exercises.”

Some red‑band students shifted guiltily. I mentally promised the Archive I would try not to crash anything. It mentally promised to log it when I did.

“At the end of each term,” Olivia said, “Houses are ranked. Higher ranks receive certain privilages. Lower ranks receive reminders.”

.

“Participate,” Olivia continued. “Show up. Don’t throw matches. Don’t injure your peers for pride. Use competitions to sharpen skills, not to prove that your ego has sharper teeth than theirs.”

She checked a slate near her lectern.

“Orientation will drown you in information,” she said. “You won’t retain all of it. That’s fine. Retain the core.

The corners of her mouth tugged up a fraction. It was not quite a smile. More like a mildly amused threat.

“Speaking of being dragged,” she added, “you have your first classes in less than twenty minutes. Schedules should have been delivered to your bands. Routes are mapped. If you get lost between here and your lecture hall, we will assume the Fog has eaten your sense of direction and act accordingly.”

A soft ripple of laughter broke.

“Last note,” Olivia said. “You are now under Institute jurisdiction. That means halls, domes, dorms, mess, and outer rings. If your band denies access, respect it. If you try to override it, disciplinary protocols will apply. Some of them are designed by Forge students with vividly creative minds.”

Her band pulsed once, scarlet.

“Welcome,” she finished. “Work hard. Do not make us regret the budget.”

She stepped back. Rian returned to the centre just long enough to give the hall a small, precise nod.

“Whatever story you wrote to get here,” he said, “this place is where you find out if it can survive contact with reality. Don’t be boring.”

The Institute crest dimmed slightly overhead. A soft chime rolled through the hall—a dismissal note, administrative and absolute.

Chairs scraped. Bands winked as people moved. Voices surged back, now rearranged around new words: House Aster, Leviathan, Meridian, Forge.

I stayed seated for a moment.

[Schedule loaded.] the Archive announced.

“Show me,” I thought.

A tab unfolded.

[ Subject: Foundation of Eidolon Theory

Instructor: Dr. Serin Ilias.

Location: Central Academic Wing, Lecture Hall 3.]

The Archive said [Recommendation: Reduced walking pace. Avoid stairs where possible. Secondary recommendation: Do not attempt to impress classmates by collapsing dramatically.]

“No guarantees,” I muttered.

I pushed myself up from the last‑row chair.

“Let’s go shall we?”
